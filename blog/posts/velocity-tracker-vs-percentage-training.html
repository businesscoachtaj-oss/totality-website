Do You Need a $2,000 Velocity Tracker to Get Stronger? New Research Says No

16 September 2026  ·  6 min read  ·  Totality Reports

If you've spent any time in a serious strength gym or on training Instagram lately, you've probably seen the little sensors clipped to barbells, the laser units bolted to power racks, or the camera rigs promising to measure every rep's bar speed in real time. Velocity-based training (VBT) has become one of the most heavily marketed "upgrades" in strength and conditioning, with hardware running anywhere from a few hundred dollars for a basic accelerometer up to roughly $2,000 for camera-based systems like Perch, plus annual software fees on top of that. The pitch is simple: your 1RM changes day to day, so why train off a fixed percentage when the barbell itself can tell you exactly how much weight belongs on it today?

Three studies published or indexed on PubMed in the past several weeks put that pitch to the test, and the picture that emerges is more interesting, and more useful for the average coach and lifter, than "buy this device."

What the new research actually found

The most direct evidence comes from a study published September 12, 2026 in the European Journal of Applied Physiology. Researchers at the University of Lisbon's Neuromuscular Research Lab took 19 trained young men and split them into two groups for 10 weeks of bench press training. One group trained with standard percentage-based training (PBT), load prescribed as a fixed percentage of their 1RM, the way most programs have worked for decades. The other trained with velocity-based training (VBT), where the load was adjusted session to session according to each lifter's individual load-velocity profile, measured with bar-speed tracking.

Both groups performed a similar number of repetitions. The researchers tracked pectoralis major cross-sectional area at three sites using imaging, along with 1RM strength, maximal isometric force, and muscle activation via EMG. After 10 weeks, both groups grew their chest muscle by roughly 15% and improved their bench 1RM by comparable amounts. There was no meaningful difference between groups in hypertrophy, strength, or muscle activation. The one measurable difference was that the VBT group spent less time under tension during the eccentric (lowering) portion of the lift, but that difference didn't translate into any advantage. The authors' conclusion was blunt: for the same number of reps performed, adjusting load based on bar velocity "did not yield hypertrophic advantage" over simply working off percentages.

That's one study, so zoom out. A network meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Physiology in June 2026 pooled 27 randomized controlled trials and 694 participants comparing five different load-prescription methods against each other: plain percentage-based training, velocity-based training, variable resistance training (bands or chains added to a barbell), APRE (a structured autoregulation protocol that adjusts load based on how many reps you complete at a given weight), and RPE/RIR-based autoregulation, training by a numerical effort scale like "leave 2 reps in reserve."

For maximal strength (back squat 1RM), variable resistance training, APRE, and VBT all outperformed plain percentage-based training. But plain RPE-based autoregulation did not show a significant advantage over percentages at all. For explosive power (jump height), APRE and VBT again came out on top, while RPE-based autoregulation actually ranked dead last of the five methods in the statistical ranking. In other words, the fanciest-sounding, most "intuitive" approach, training by feel, was the weakest performer for strength and power outcomes when it wasn't backed by a structured protocol or objective data.

A third study, published in Frontiers in Physiology in February 2026, tested this same idea in a different context: blood flow restriction (BFR) training. Researchers had 21 resistance-trained men do BFR leg training for 8 weeks, with one leg trained under a device that automatically adjusted cuff pressure in real time (autoregulated) and the other leg under a fixed, properly calibrated pressure. Muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, and strength gains were statistically identical between legs. Adding real-time autoregulating hardware to an already effective method added nothing measurable.

Why this matters for your programming

Put together, these three studies tell a more precise story than "autoregulation doesn't work" or "gadgets are useless." The nuance matters: autoregulating your training, adjusting load to how your body is actually performing that day, is still a sound principle. What these studies undercut is the assumption that the specific tool you use to do it, especially an expensive one, is what drives the result. For pure hypertrophy, matching total volume and effort mattered far more than whether the load came from a percentage chart or a velocity sensor. For strength and power, structure beat vibes: a defined protocol (APRE) or an external load manipulation (bands and chains) outperformed unstructured "go by feel" RPE, and untracked RPE was consistently the weakest of the five methods tested.

That last point is the one coaches should sit with. A lot of trainers already believe they're autoregulating because they ask clients "how did that feel?" at the end of a set. The research suggests that loose, unlogged feel-based adjustment is doing the least work of any method studied. The value in RPE/RIR training doesn't come from asking the question. It comes from consistently capturing the answer as a number, every set, so you can see the trend over weeks and catch fatigue or a stalling program before a lifter plateaus or gets hurt.

What to actually do this week

If you're a lifter deciding whether to buy a velocity tracker: based on this evidence, you can save the money for hypertrophy-focused training. A well-run percentage-based or logged-RIR program will get you comparable muscle growth without the hardware.

If you're chasing raw strength or explosive power specifically, the current evidence favors either adding variable resistance (bands or chains on your main lifts) or following a structured autoregulation protocol like APRE rather than training purely off unlogged feel.

If you coach clients on RPE or RIR, tighten up how it's used. Set a specific target every session (for example, "work up to a set of 5 at RIR 2"), have the client report a number immediately after the set rather than estimating from memory later, and track that number over time. The studies suggest structured, logged autoregulation is doing real work, while unstructured "how'd that feel" is closer to noise.

If you already use BFR training, don't feel pressure to upgrade to an autoregulating cuff. A correctly calibrated fixed-pressure setup produces the same results in the research so far.

The broader lesson: the newest tool isn't automatically the better tool. The studies above measured people who trained with real discipline regardless of method. The discipline of showing up, tracking numbers, and progressing systematically is what the data keeps rewarding, gadget or no gadget.

Note on limitations: the lead hypertrophy study had a modest sample size (n=19) typical of controlled training trials of this length, and studied trained young men specifically, so results may not generalize to untrained lifters or other populations. The network meta-analysis pooled studies of varying quality and design, and its authors caution that rankings should be interpreted with care given between-study heterogeneity.
