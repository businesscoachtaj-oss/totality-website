Collagen vs. Whey: What a New Meta-Analysis Actually Shows (and Why the Field's Biggest Names Are Pushing Back)

September 9, 2026  ·  Totality

If you scroll past a supplement ad this week, there's a decent chance it's selling collagen as the "smarter" muscle-building protein, and for once, it isn't just marketing spin making that claim. A new network meta-analysis published in Translational Sports Medicine found that, across 78 studies and nearly 5,000 participants, collagen supplementation outperformed whey protein (the reigning champion of sports nutrition for two decades) for both strength gains and increases in fat-free mass in adults doing resistance training.

That's a genuinely startling result. It's also, according to some of the most respected protein researchers in the field, a result that doesn't hold up well once you look under the hood. This week's deep dive is about that tension: what the study found, why it made headlines, and why the more careful read is "don't throw out your whey tub yet," with a clear, practical takeaway for anyone training or coaching clients.

What the study actually did

The research, led by Marcos Drummond and colleagues at the Federal University of Minas Gerais in Brazil, is a network meta-analysis, a statistical method that lets researchers compare multiple treatments (in this case, 13 different types of protein supplements, plus placebo and no-supplement controls) even when most of the underlying trials never compared those treatments head-to-head. Instead, the method builds an indirect comparison network by chaining together studies that share a common reference point, like "Supplement A vs. placebo" and "Supplement B vs. placebo," to estimate how A and B would likely compare to each other.

The team pooled 78 randomized controlled trials with 4,755 total participants, all involving healthy adults doing structured resistance training alongside a protein supplement. They measured two outcomes: muscle strength (the primary outcome, mostly via 1-rep-max style lifts) and fat-free mass (essentially, lean body mass measured by DEXA or similar).

The headline results: compared to placebo, collagen produced the largest effect on strength (standardized mean difference of 0.41, a small-to-moderate effect) and by far the largest effect on fat-free mass (SMD of 0.94, a large effect), outranking whey protein, which showed smaller but still statistically significant benefits on both measures (SMD 0.15 for strength, 0.16 for fat-free mass). The other 11 supplement categories tested, including casein, soy, pea, and various blends, showed no statistically significant advantage over placebo at all. In the study's ranking system (called SUCRA, which expresses how likely a treatment is to be the best option), collagen scored 88–99% depending on the outcome, versus roughly 60–64% for whey.

Why the researchers who know this space best are skeptical

Within weeks of publication, a formal rebuttal appeared in the same journal from Stuart Phillips, a McMaster University exercise and nutrition scientist whose lab has spent over 20 years defining how the body uses protein to build muscle, and whose name shows up on a large share of the foundational research this field relies on. His critique, along with independent analysis from other science writers covering the paper, raises several concrete problems worth understanding rather than dismissing as academic nitpicking:

The collagen result rests on a thin evidence base. Despite the network pulling from 78 total studies, collagen's standout ranking is driven by just four small trials, each with fewer than 30 participants, and at least two of them appear to draw on overlapping datasets, meaning the "independent" evidence is thinner than the headline number suggests. When critics reran the analysis with just one of those four studies removed, collagen's statistically significant edge disappeared.

There's a funding wrinkle. At least one of the underlying collagen trials was funded by a collagen-peptide manufacturer (Gelita), with study authors reporting financial ties to the company. That doesn't automatically invalidate the data, but it's the kind of detail that belongs in the conversation whenever a supplement category posts a surprise win.

The biology is still a real obstacle. Collagen is, chemically speaking, an "incomplete" protein: it's very low in tryptophan and contains meaningfully less leucine and total essential amino acid content per serving than whey (commonly cited figures put whey's essential amino acid content at roughly 13-14g per 20-25g serving, versus around 7-8g for a comparable collagen serving). Leucine in particular is the amino acid that most directly triggers muscle protein synthesis, and whey has consistently out-performed collagen on that specific, well-studied mechanism in controlled lab feeding trials. A single splashy statistical ranking doesn't erase decades of that mechanistic evidence.

Almost none of the underlying trials directly compared collagen to whey in the same study. Because this is a network (indirect) meta-analysis, the "collagen beats whey" conclusion is inferred by triangulating through placebo-controlled trials rather than measured in trials that put the two supplements head-to-head in the same room. Indirect comparisons are a legitimate and useful statistical tool, but they carry more uncertainty than a real head-to-head trial, and that uncertainty doesn't always come through in a tidy summary table.

None of this means the Brazilian team's meta-analysis was done in bad faith. Network meta-analyses are a standard, respected tool, and the authors were transparent about their methods and included the caveat that "gaps remain" in direct comparisons. But it's a good real-world example of why a single new paper, however well-designed, rarely overturns an entire field overnight, and why the response from other experts matters as much as the original result.

Why this matters for people training, and for coaches advising them

For your own training or your clients' programming, the practical stakes here are lower than the headlines suggest, and that's actually the useful takeaway. A few things are true at the same time:

Collagen isn't useless for people who train. Separate, well-established research shows collagen peptides can meaningfully support tendon and ligament resilience, and may help with joint comfort in people managing joint-related training limitations. That's a real, evidence-backed lane for collagen that has nothing to do with this new muscle-strength claim.

Whey (and other complete, leucine-rich proteins like dairy, eggs, or a well-formulated plant blend) remains the better-evidenced default for anyone whose primary goal is maximizing muscle protein synthesis and strength adaptation from resistance training. The mechanistic case for leucine-rich complete proteins hasn't been overturned by one paper with a thin evidence base behind its most eye-catching claim.

The single biggest lever, by far, is still total daily protein intake matched to training volume: most sports-nutrition guidance continues to land in the 1.6-2.2g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day range for people doing regular resistance training, spread across multiple meals. Which supplement you use to help hit that number matters far less than actually hitting it consistently.

If a client already likes collagen (for skin, joints, gut comfort, or simply preference), there's no strong reason to talk them out of it, especially if they're also getting adequate total protein and leucine from other sources across the day. But if a client is choosing collagen instead of whey specifically because they read that it "builds more muscle," that's the moment for a coach to gently correct the record with what's above.

What to do this week

If you're training: keep your primary protein source built around complete, leucine-rich options (whey, dairy, eggs, meat, or a leucine-fortified plant blend) and treat collagen as an optional add-on for joint and connective-tissue support rather than a strength-building substitute. Don't swap your post-workout shake based on this one study.

If you're coaching clients: use this as a live example of how to read supplement research with clients. A good talking point is "one exciting new study rarely changes the picture on its own; look at whether it's been checked by other experts, and whether it fits what we already know about how the body works." That habit builds more trust than either uncritically repeating headlines or dismissing new research outright.

Either way, the actionable number to track this week isn't which protein powder is in your shaker. It's whether you're actually hitting your daily protein target, meal by meal, consistently. That's the variable the evidence agrees on.
