Full-Body or Split? What the Newest Research Actually Says About Training Frequency and Volume

September 9, 2026  ·  Totality

If you coach clients, or you're the client squeezing workouts around a job, kids and everything else life throws at you, you've probably had this argument with yourself at least once: should you hit each muscle group more often in shorter sessions, or is it fine to smash everything hard once a week? It's one of the oldest debates in strength training, and for just as long the answer has mostly come down to bro-science and gym folklore.

This week, three pieces of evidence, two brand-new studies published in the first week of September 2026, and a landmark evidence synthesis from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), line up to give a genuinely useful, plain-English answer. None of it is flashy. All of it is directly usable in your programming this week.

Myth-busting #1: does training more muscle "steal" gains from muscle you already trained?

A common worry, especially among people doing full-body or upper/lower splits, is that working a lot of muscle mass in one session creates "competition" for a limited pool of recovery resources (protein synthesis, hormones, energy), leaving less for the muscle you actually care about growing.

A new randomized trial out of the University of Mississippi's Kevser Ermin Applied Physiology Laboratory, published in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, put this directly to the test. Researchers took 105 untrained adults and split them into three groups: one did low-load elbow-flexion (bicep curl) exercise to failure only; a second did the identical arm protocol plus four extra sets of low-load knee-extension exercise to failure on each leg; and a third did no training at all as a control. Everyone trained twice a week for six weeks under normal, unsupervised eating conditions.

If the "competition for resources" idea were true, the group also training their legs should have shown blunted arm growth compared with the arms-only group. That's not what happened. Upper-arm muscle thickness increased almost identically in both training groups (0.19cm vs. 0.18cm), and strength gains in the trained arm were statistically indistinguishable between groups too. The researchers used Bayesian statistics, rather than a simple significance test, specifically so they could quantify evidence for "no difference," not just fail to find one, and the data leaned clearly toward no competition effect (Kataoka et al., 2026, J Strength Cond Res).

Caveats worth flagging for your own critical read: these were untrained participants doing low-load, to-failure protocols over just six weeks, not the same as an advanced lifter juggling high-volume leg days and a hypertrophy-focused arm block. The authors themselves note that under a genuine calorie deficit, or at much higher training volumes, a resource-competition effect might still show up. But as a starting-point finding, it undercuts a piece of programming folklore that keeps people needlessly splintering their weekly volume across more sessions than they need.

Myth-busting #2: does spreading sets across more sessions per week grow more muscle?

The second new study, from researchers at the State University of Campinas in Brazil (published September 8 in the same journal), tackles the flip side of the frequency question: if you keep the same weekly number of hard sets, does it matter whether you cram them into one heavy session or spread them across three?

Eighteen trained men did six weeks of lower-body training (back squat, leg press, leg extension), with three weekly sets per exercise taken to concentric failure at 80% of their one-rep max. One group did all three sets in a single weekly session; the other spread one set across three separate sessions. The spread-out group could complete significantly more total volume load (58% more on the back squat, 76% more on the leg press) simply because fresher muscles let them move more weight per set. Both groups gained meaningful strength and quadriceps muscle thickness over the six weeks, and while the higher-frequency group showed a trend toward greater strength gains on squat and leg press, it didn't reach statistical significance (p = 0.074 and 0.071), and there was no meaningful difference in muscle thickness gains at all between the two approaches (Calzavara & Barroso, 2026, J Strength Cond Res).

In other words: more frequency let these lifters do more work, but doing more work didn't clearly translate into more muscle over just six weeks. That's an important, and often overlooked, distinction between volume load (a number on a spreadsheet) and the training stimulus that actually drives adaptation.

The bigger picture: ACSM's 2026 position stand

Both of these fresh studies land inside a much larger, more authoritative piece of context: ACSM's newly updated Position Stand on resistance training prescription, an "overview of reviews" that pooled findings from 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants, the first major update to ACSM's resistance training guidance since 2009 (Currier et al., 2026, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise).

Its conclusions map neatly onto what the two new studies are showing at a smaller scale. Hypertrophy is driven mainly by total weekly training volume: the review specifically flags ten or more sets per muscle per week, plus eccentric-overload work, as the levers that reliably move the needle on muscle size. Strength, by contrast, responds best to heavier loads (80% of 1RM or above), full range of motion, two to three sets per exercise done early in a session, and training that muscle at least twice a week, frequency matters more for strength than for size. Meanwhile, a long list of things many lifters obsess over, training to complete muscular failure, specific periodization schemes, exercise order complexity, time under tension, and equipment type, did not consistently move outcomes one way or the other across the pooled evidence.

Put simply: volume is king for size, frequency mostly matters for strength rather than muscle growth, and a lot of the fine-tuning lifters and coaches agonize over has surprisingly little evidence behind it.

What this means for your programming this week

For clients or lifters who are time-poor: don't feel obligated to chop your week into more sessions purely to unlock frequency benefits for hypertrophy. If total weekly volume per muscle is similar, two well-loaded full-body sessions can hold their own against a four- or five-day split for muscle growth; frequency's real payoff shows up more in strength outcomes.

For anyone chasing pure strength numbers, a 1RM goal, a powerlifting meet, general functional strength, frequency is where you should actually spend extra sessions. Hitting a lift twice a week rather than once, with two to three quality sets near 80%+ of 1RM early in the session, is the more defensible way to spend limited training time if strength, not size, is the target.

For coaches building programmes: this is a good week to sanity-check whether a client's split exists because the evidence supports it, or because it's just how you've always written programmes. If a client's total weekly hard sets per muscle are already at or above roughly ten, chasing extra frequency purely for size probably isn't worth the added session count. And if someone's anxious that a heavy leg day will cannibalise their upper-body gains, the newest evidence says that's very unlikely to be the limiting factor: poor total volume, poor recovery, or inadequate nutrition are far more likely culprits.

None of this is a reason to throw out a programme that's working. It's a reason to double-check that the variables you're manipulating, split structure, session frequency, how sets are distributed, are actually the ones the evidence says matter for the goal you're chasing.
