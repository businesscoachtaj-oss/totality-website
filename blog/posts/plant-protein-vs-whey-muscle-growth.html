Plant Protein vs Whey for Muscle Growth: What Two New Reviews Found

26 November 2026  ·  7 min read  ·  Totality Reports

Most people who buy a plant protein powder stand in the supplement aisle for a while first. They have heard that whey is the gold standard. They have also heard that pea and soy are just as good now, or nearly as good, or good enough if you take more. The labels do not settle it, and neither, for a long time, did the research.

Two systematic reviews published in October 2026 in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition come at the question from opposite ends. One looks at what happens inside a muscle in the few hours after a single workout. The other looks at what happens to people's bodies over months of training. Neither is the last word. Together they are the clearest picture we have had of how plant protein compares with whey for building muscle.

The long view: five trials in people new to lifting

Irineu Callegari and colleagues at São Paulo State University went looking for randomised trials that compared a plant protein supplement directly against whey, in healthy adults aged 18 to 59 who had not lifted weights before, over at least six weeks of resistance training. They also insisted that body composition be measured by DEXA scan, which is more precise than the bioimpedance scales found in many gyms. That is a narrow gate, and only five trials made it through, with 372 participants between them.

Three of the trials used soy protein, one used pea protein and one used chia. They ran from eight weeks to nine months. When the results were pooled, the people taking plant protein and the people taking whey gained similar amounts of lean mass and lost similar amounts of fat. The pooled difference in lean mass sat close to zero, and the individual trials did not disagree with each other much.

The details inside those trials are worth a moment. In one, 19 grams of whey isolate was compared with 26 grams of soy isolate, an amount chosen so that both servings delivered about 2 grams of leucine, the amino acid most closely tied to switching on muscle building. Lean mass and strength gains were comparable. In a 12 week trial in sedentary adults aged 30 to 59, pea protein kept pace with whey. The single chia trial found no meaningful change in lean mass with either supplement, in young men who were already eating more than 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day. And the longest trial, which ran for nine months, found that whey did produce more lean mass than soy.

The authors are careful about what this means, and it is worth being careful with them. Five trials is a small evidence base. The confidence intervals were wide, which means a modest advantage for either protein could still be hiding in the data. They searched only three databases, which they list as a limitation. They write plainly that their result should not be read as proof that plant protein and whey are physiologically equivalent. What they can say is that, in people starting out, soy and pea supplements were associated with similar changes in body composition to whey across the trials that exist.

The short view: 51 studies of muscle building after one session

The second review, led by Kevin Bischof at the University of Vienna, took a different approach. It gathered 51 studies, covering 1,128 participants, in which people did a single resistance training session, drank a single dose of protein, and had small samples of thigh muscle taken before and afterwards. Researchers then measured how quickly new muscle protein was being built over the following two to seven hours.

Because most of these studies compared only one or two proteins, the team used a network meta-analysis, a method that lets you rank many options against each other even when they were never tested side by side. Nineteen protein sources ended up in the network, from whey and casein to beef, salmon, eggs, soy, pea, potato, fava bean, spirulina, mealworm and mycoprotein, the fungus-based protein used in some meat alternatives.

The top of the ranking was mixed. Native whey (a less processed form of whey) came first, followed by pea protein, whole egg protein, mycoprotein and regular whey. Only regular whey showed an effect that was statistically clear on its own, largely because it had by far the most studies behind it, 37 study arms against 2 for pea and 1 for native whey. Fava bean and mealworm protein sat at the bottom.

The dose analysis is where the practical detail lives. Native whey produced its strongest predicted response at around 20 grams and whole egg protein at around 18 grams, while pea protein and mycoprotein reached comparable rankings at around 25 grams. The authors suggest this fits with what is already known about plant proteins: they tend to be a little less digestible and lower in leucine, so a slightly larger serve may be needed to get the same signal. They also note that they did not formally test dose against dose, and they describe this finding as one to be confirmed rather than relied on.

The limits here are real. The studies varied a great deal from one to the next, and the statistics showed some inconsistency in the network, mostly around casein. Most participants were young. And a burst of muscle building in the hours after one workout is not the same thing as muscle growth over a year. Whether the first reliably predicts the second is still debated.

Whether a protein powder helps at all

It is a fair thing to wonder before choosing between powders. A third meta-analysis from October 2026, by Chenkun Zhang and colleagues in Frontiers in Nutrition, pooled 23 randomised trials with 611 healthy young adults. Adding a protein supplement to resistance training produced about 2.6 kilograms more on the bench press and about 7.4 kilograms more on the squat than training alone. The benefit was not universal. It showed up mainly in programmes of 12 weeks or less, and the authors think it depends on how much protein people were already eating. If your daily intake is already high, a shake may add little.

What this means if you are choosing a protein

Taken together, these reviews point in the same direction. For most people who lift, the source of their protein matters less than whether they eat enough of it, spread across the day, alongside training they actually do. Pea and soy look like reasonable choices. Whey still has the deepest evidence base, and the one long trial in the set leaned its way.

  • If you prefer plant protein, pea and soy have the most support. The evidence for chia, hemp, fava bean and other single sources is thin or less encouraging.
  • Consider a slightly bigger serve. Around 25 grams of pea protein ranked alongside about 20 grams of whey in the acute studies. Check the label for grams of protein per scoop, which is often less than the scoop weight.
  • Whole foods count too. Firm tofu, dried peas and mycoprotein products all contribute, as do eggs if you eat them.
  • Daily total comes first. A powder is a convenient way to reach a target. It is not a requirement, and it adds less if you already eat plenty of protein.
  • Older lifters may need more care. Most of this research was done in younger adults, and older muscle tends to respond less to the same dose of protein.

There is something quietly reassuring in all of this. The choice in the supplement aisle feels heavy, and the research suggests it is lighter than it looks. Pick the protein you can afford, digest well and will keep taking, then put your attention on the training and the meals that surround it.
