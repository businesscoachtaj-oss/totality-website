This guide is for trainers who know the basics from Certificate III and IV: the food groups, macros, and the Australian Dietary Guidelines. It goes into the research behind the numbers, where popular advice has drifted from the science, and where a trainer's scope ends.

Nutrition is also where a well meaning trainer can do harm: overselling a supplement, handing out a rigid meal plan, or giving advice they aren't qualified or insured for. This guide takes that as seriously as the science.

:::box A note on accuracy
Strong, consistent findings are stated plainly. Mixed or early findings are marked as such.
:::

# Energy balance

Calories in versus calories out holds. The mistake is treating a client's daily energy use (TDEE) as a known number. It's an estimate from an equation, compared with food logs that are rarely as accurate as they feel.

## Choosing an equation

**Mifflin St Jeor** is the most accurate on average for most adults, ahead of the older Harris Benedict. **Katch McArdle** uses lean mass and can suit very lean or muscular clients, but only if the body fat estimate is good. Even the best equation is off by about 10 to 15% for an individual. Use it to start, then adjust from two to three weeks of real weight trend.

## Metabolic adaptation

When people diet, their metabolism drops a little more than their lower body weight explains. The well known extreme examples come from very fast, very large weight loss. For a client losing around half a kilo a week the effect is small, partly metabolism and partly moving less without noticing (lower NEAT, the activity outside exercise).

## Burning fat and losing fat

Burning fat means using it for fuel, which everyone does all day. Losing fat means less stored fat over weeks, decided by energy balance. Focus on the week, not the fuel used in one session.

:::box Diet breaks
Planned weeks at maintenance can make a diet easier to stick to. Evidence that they protect muscle or metabolism more than the same total calories eaten steadily is thin. Use them for adherence.
:::

# Protein

Protein has the clearest dose and response research in nutrition, so use real numbers.

| Client | Daily protein |
|---|---|
| Training for muscle | 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight. Little extra gain above about 1.6 for most people |
| Lean and trained, in a deficit | 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of lean mass, to protect muscle |
| Older adults | At least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg, up to 1.5 with illness or injury, about 25 to 30 g each meal |
| Not training | 0.8 g per kg is the minimum to avoid deficiency, not a training target |

## Timing

Total daily protein matters far more than eating it within an hour of training. Spread it over three or four meals of about 25 to 40 g, which gives enough leucine at each meal to switch on muscle building.

## Protein with carbs

Adding carbs to an adequate protein dose doesn't build more muscle. Carbs with protein after training is still a practical way to refuel and hit targets.

## Plant protein

Vegetarian and vegan clients don't need to combine proteins at each meal. Enough total protein and variety across the day is what counts.

# Carbs, fat and popular diets

Once protein and calories match, most diet debates come down to preference and what someone will stick with.

- **Carbs** fuel training and the brain. Very low carb can hurt training even when the scale looks fine. Carbs aren't fattening on their own.
- **Low fat or low carb:** a 12 month trial of 609 people found no meaningful difference in weight loss between a healthy low fat and a healthy low carb diet. Keto studies in trained people show similar body composition to calorie matched diets.
- **Fat** below about 20% of calories, especially with low total intake, is linked with lower reproductive hormones. Keep it above that when dieting hard.
- **Fibre** of 25 to 30 g a day is linked with lower rates of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer. Most clients eat well under this, so it's often a more useful conversation than macros.

:::box A better question
Ask "which way of eating could you keep up for six months?" rather than "which diet is best?"
:::

# Meal timing and fasting

- **Meal frequency:** six small meals don't raise metabolism compared with three larger ones at the same total. Choose for hunger and schedule.
- **Intermittent fasting:** in a well controlled trial, 16:8 eating gave no extra weight loss compared with normal eating times, and a little more lean mass loss. Reviews describe fasting as a way to eat less. Fine for a client who finds it easier, not a better default.
- **Workout timing** matters more for elite and high volume athletes. For most clients, hitting daily protein and calories matters far more.

# Hydration and supplements

## Hydration

There's no strong evidence for eight glasses a day. Needs vary with body size, climate, sweat and food. Drinking to thirst covers most clients.

## Supplements by evidence

| Evidence | Supplements |
|---|---|
| Strong | Creatine monohydrate, 3 to 5 g a day, no cycling. Caffeine, about 3 to 6 mg per kg, 30 to 60 minutes before training. Protein powder as a convenient way to hit protein |
| Promising | Fish oil, curcumin, most polyphenols. Fine to try, don't oversell |
| Weak at usual doses | BCAAs (whole protein does the job better), most fat burners, extra amino acids on top of enough protein |
| Don't recommend | Prohormones, unregulated testosterone boosters, anything banned or high risk for contamination |

Creatine is the most researched, safest and best value supplement for most clients.

# Why plans fail

A perfect plan a client drops after two weeks does less than an imperfect one they follow for six months.

- **Flexible beats rigid.** All or nothing rules ("never eat this", "one bad meal ruins the day") are linked with a higher risk of disordered eating. This matters most for perfectionist or anxious clients.
- **Tracking helps, with care.** Logging closes the gap between what people think they eat and what they do. For a few clients, especially anyone with a history of disordered eating, it can become obsessive. Watch for it.
- **Habits beat willpower.** A set breakfast, a standing grocery list, a go to dinner for busy nights. One or two habits a client keeps beat a full plan they abandon.

# Know your scope

## What you can coach

- Healthy eating based on the Australian Dietary Guidelines: food groups, serves and portions.
- General weight management through energy balance, food quality and habits.
- Reading food labels, and examples of balanced meals and snacks.

## What belongs with an Accredited Practising Dietitian

- Meal plans for medical conditions such as diabetes, coeliac disease, allergies, or kidney or liver disease.
- Detailed prescriptions for complex cases, and any question about medicines and food.
- Specialised or restrictive diets. Detailed help moving to vegetarian or vegan eating is closer to this line than most trainers think.

:::box Refer, don't coach
Refer to a dietitian for any diagnosed condition, signs of disordered eating, a client fixed on extreme restriction, or a client not progressing despite following general advice. "Dietitian" isn't a protected title in Australia, so look for an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) through Dietitians Australia.
:::

# Different clients

- **Vegetarian and vegan:** enough protein is very doable. Watch vitamin B12 (not in plant foods), iron and zinc (absorbed less from plants) and creatine (almost absent). They often respond well to creatine.
- **Energy availability:** Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) means eating too little for the training load. It can affect periods, bones, metabolism and performance in anyone, not only athletes. Watch for it in anyone training hard while under eating, especially with irregular or absent periods. Changes in appetite across the menstrual cycle are real for some people, but not consistent enough to plan around by default.
- **Older adults:** need more protein, at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg, with 25 to 30 g at each main meal, because older muscle responds less to each dose.

# Common nutrition coaching mistakes

?? Treating a TDEE number as exact :: It's an estimate. Adjust from the real weight trend.
?? Timing over totals :: Daily protein and calories matter far more than workout windows.
?? Restrictive diets by default :: A flexible plan they follow beats a strict one they can't.
?? Supplements before basics :: No fat burner beats fixing protein, calories and consistency.
?? Medical nutrition advice :: Meal plans for conditions and medicine questions go to a dietitian.
?? One rule for everyone :: A vegan client, a client in perimenopause and a 70 year old have different priorities.

# Your nutrition coaching checklist

- [ ] Estimate energy needs with Mifflin St Jeor, then adjust from two to three weeks of weight trend
- [ ] Set protein by goal: 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg, 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg lean mass when cutting, 1.0 to 1.5 g/kg for older adults
- [ ] Spread protein over three or four meals
- [ ] Choose a way of eating the client can keep, once protein and calories match
- [ ] Keep fat above about 20% of calories and aim for 25 to 30 g of fibre
- [ ] Use meal timing and fasting for adherence, not metabolism
- [ ] Recommend drinking to thirst
- [ ] Lead supplement advice with creatine and caffeine
- [ ] Avoid rigid all or nothing plans
- [ ] Watch for tracking becoming obsessive
- [ ] Stay in scope and refer to an APD when you see a red flag
- [ ] Check B12, iron, zinc and creatine for vegetarian and vegan clients
- [ ] Watch for under eating in anyone training hard
- [ ] Coach the person in front of you, not one rule for everyone
