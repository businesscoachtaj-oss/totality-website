This guide is for trainers who know the basics from Certificate III and IV and want the reasons behind programming decisions. It covers the evidence, where it's strong and where it's mixed, and how to use it with a real client.

Two ideas run through it. The textbook principles are where you start, and two clients with the same goal can still need different programmes. And where a popular claim is weaker than it sounds, this guide says so.

:::box A note on accuracy
Exercise science keeps moving. Strong, consistent findings are stated plainly. Mixed or early findings are marked as such.
:::

# What a programme is

A workout is one session. A programme is a plan over weeks, where each session sets up the next. A client can train hard every session and still not be on a programme if nothing changes on purpose.

## The loop

1. **Assess:** ability, goals, training history, injuries and limits, before you write anything.
2. **Plan:** sessions and weeks that build toward the goal.
3. **Run it:** coach the session as written, and coach the person in front of you.
4. **Track:** loads, reps, how hard it felt, how they recovered.
5. **Adjust:** change the plan from what really happened.

New clients improve for months on almost any sensible training. After that early phase, progress depends on whether the plan builds on itself.

:::box A quick check
If nothing is clearly harder than four weeks ago (more weight, reps or sets, or less rest), the client is repeating a workout rather than following a programme.
:::

# Progressive overload

Gradually asking more of the body over time is about as settled as exercise science gets.

## More weight is one of several ways

- More reps at the same weight
- More sets
- Less rest between sets
- A harder variation or more range of motion
- The same weight and reps with better control

## Over a block, not every session

Overload should show across weeks. Forcing more weight every session regardless of recovery leads to burnout. A beginner can keep adapting to the same work for a while because it's new to them.

:::box Example
A client has used the same 10 kg dumbbells for eight weeks and nothing has moved. The fix usually isn't heavier dumbbells straight away. It's one more rep, or one more set, next week.
:::

# Volume, frequency and intensity

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| Volume | How much work: sets × reps, or total weight lifted |
| Intensity | How hard: a percentage of one rep max, or effort (RPE or reps in reserve) |
| Frequency | How often a muscle or movement is trained each week |

## Volume

More weekly sets are generally linked with more muscle growth, up to a point. You may have heard of MEV, MAV and MRV (minimum, adaptive and maximum recoverable volume). The idea behind them is sound, but the exact set numbers are a coaching rule of thumb, not tested limits. They vary between people and from week to week.

## Frequency

With the same weekly volume, training a muscle **twice a week** gives better results than once a week. Going higher than that shows no strong extra benefit on its own. Use extra days to spread the volume so no session is too long.

## Intensity

Exact percentages are hard to use with most clients, whose strength changes day to day. **Reps in reserve (RIR)**, how many more reps they could have done, is simpler. On an off day a client can hit the same RIR with a lighter weight and still get a good session.

:::box Example
12 weekly sets of squats split over two sessions (6 and 6) is easier to recover from, and just as effective, as 12 in one session.
:::

# Periodisation

Periodisation means planning how volume and intensity change over time.

| Model | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | Higher reps and lighter loads, moving to lower reps and heavier loads over a block | Beginners making steady progress |
| Undulating | Heavy, moderate and light days within each week | Trained lifters chasing strength. Little advantage for beginners or for muscle growth |
| Block | Set phases, such as muscle, then strength, then peaking | Competition or a set date |

## Deloads

A lighter week every four to eight weeks is sensible fatigue management. It hasn't been tested on its own the way overload has, but periodised programmes, which build in lighter phases, do beat non-periodised ones for strength.

# Fit it to the person

The principles are the start. A good coach reads the person, because two people with the same goal can need different programmes to stick with it.

- **Clients who like routine** are motivated by numbers going up on the same lifts. Keep the main lifts the same for weeks at a time.
- **Clients who like variety** lose interest with repetition. Rotate accessories, tempos or equipment, but keep one or two lifts the same each session so you can still measure progress.
- **Clients training once or twice a week** shouldn't be on a body part split, because each muscle would get trained once a week or less. Do full body every session, keep a squat, hinge, push and pull the same for four to six weeks, and rotate the rest more freely.

:::box The best programme
The best programme is the one a person will do, consistently, for months. A perfect plan they drop after three weeks gets worse results than a good one they keep.
:::

# Training men and women

Some claims about sex differences hold up. Others stretch a real finding too far.

## Well established

- **Absolute strength and power:** men are usually stronger in absolute terms, mostly from more muscle mass and more fast twitch fibre.
- **Strength for size:** strength per amount of muscle is about the same. A smaller, well trained woman can be just as strong for her size.
- **ACL injury:** women have roughly twice the ACL injury rate of men in similar sports, linked to landing mechanics. Include landing technique and posterior chain strength work for clients who jump or change direction.

## Fatigue, more precisely

Women do resist fatigue better in sustained, moderate, mostly static efforts. That doesn't extend cleanly to heavy lifting or next day recovery, where some studies find the opposite. Don't set volume or frequency by sex. Training history, recovery and how someone responds tell you more.

## The menstrual cycle

Reviews describe the evidence for planning training around cycle phases as inconsistent. It's fine to talk about with a client who wants to track it, but not a default rule.

# From plan to session

## The shape of a session

1. **Warm up:** general movement, then ramp up sets for the main lift.
2. **Main lift:** the day's main movement, where you track progress most closely.
3. **Accessories:** support the main lift or fill a gap.
4. **Finisher:** short conditioning, only if time and recovery allow.

## Example: three full body sessions a week (beginner)

Rotate a squat, hinge, push and pull across days A, B and C. Main lifts: **3 sets of 8 to 10 at about RPE 7** (around 3 reps in reserve). Accessories: 2 sets of 12 to 15.

Use **double progression**: add a rep each session until every set hits the top of the range, then add a little weight and go back to the bottom of the range.

## Example: one session a week

Full body each time. Keep the squat, hinge, push and pull the same for four to six weeks so there's something to track, and change the accessories each week to keep it fresh.

:::box Write it down
A session nobody can remember next week can't be built on. Record weight, reps and effort for every working set.
:::

# Common programming mistakes

?? Changing everything every session :: It feels fresh, but nothing stays the same long enough to progress or measure.
?? One template for everyone :: The same 12 week plan given to a routine lover and a variety seeker will lose one of them within a month.
?? Ignoring how they feel today :: Plans that never adjust for sleep, stress or soreness give worse sessions than effort based adjustment.
?? No deloads until an injury :: A planned light week costs less than an unplanned week off.
?? Rules by sex instead of the person :: Set volume and recovery from their history and response.
?? Smashing once a week clients :: A hard session they dread loses to a moderate one they keep coming to.

# Your programming checklist

- [ ] Assess goals, history, injuries and limits before you plan
- [ ] Make sure every block has progress you can see and track
- [ ] Train each muscle or movement at least twice a week where you can
- [ ] Use RPE or reps in reserve to adjust load on off days
- [ ] Pick a periodisation model to match experience
- [ ] Plan a lighter week every four to eight weeks
- [ ] Work out whether each client likes routine or variety
- [ ] Use full body sessions and steady main lifts for 1 to 2 day a week clients
- [ ] Add landing and posterior chain work for jumping and change of direction
- [ ] Set volume and recovery by the person, not by sex
- [ ] Give each session a shape: warm up, main lift, accessories, finisher
- [ ] Use double progression by default
- [ ] Record every working set
- [ ] Review the last four weeks before writing the next block
