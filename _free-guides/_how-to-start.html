It covers what you need on paper, what to charge, and what to have in place before your first client walks in.

Where something depends on your state, your niche or your budget, it says so.

A note on accuracy

Qualifications, registration and insurance rules change, and some vary by state. Use this as a starting point, then check the current details with AUSactive or your registration body before you spend money.

01
Chapter 1

Get qualified

In Australia the usual path is the Certificate III in Fitness (SIS30321), then the Certificate IV in Fitness (SIS40221). Certificate III qualifies you as a gym instructor. Certificate IV qualifies you as a personal trainer who designs programmes on their own. The qualification is the same across the country. Providers differ in price, how they teach, where they are and how much hands on practice you get.

Where to study

  • TAFE in your state. Usually the cheapest option, and the fitness certificates have been offered as Fee-Free TAFE places in several states. Places and eligible courses change, so check with your state's TAFE.
  • Private colleges, such as the Australian Institute of Fitness, with campuses in most capital cities and online study.
  • Smaller registered training organisations (RTOs), often regional or online only.

Online, on campus or both

Option Good for Keep in mind
Online, self paced Studying around another job Certificate IV still needs supervised practical assessment in person
On campus Hands on practice from day one Fixed timetable and location
Blended Most people Online theory with set practical days

Register, insure and get first aid

  • Register with AUSactive, the national body for exercise professionals. It isn't compulsory everywhere, but most gyms and insurers expect it, and clients can check it.
  • Insurance: public liability and professional indemnity, before you train anyone, paid or not. Gyms and registration bodies usually ask to see your certificate of currency.
  • First aid and CPR: keep both current. Insurers and gyms check them.

Specialise later

You don't need a niche to start. Most trainers coach a broad mix for their first year or two and find their niche from who they enjoy coaching and get results with. Specialties like pre and post natal or older adults usually need an extra course, so check there's demand near you first.

Chapter 2

Choose how you'll work

How you work decides how you're paid, what insurance you need and how much admin you do.

Model How it works Suits
Employed at a gym Paid a wage or salary. The gym finds clients and runs the space Building experience with low risk
Contractor at a gym You pay rent or a share and keep the rest. You do your own bookings and marketing Trainers with some clients already
Mobile or outdoor You go to the client, at home or in a park Low costs. Travel limits how many sessions fit in a day
Online coaching Programmes and check-ins from a distance Coaching more people than your hours allow
Your own studio Lease, equipment and maybe staff Experienced trainers who can bring clients with them

Training groups in a park? Many councils need a permit, so check yours.

How gym based trainers are paid

  • Wage or salary. A set rate. As a genuine employee you also get super and leave. The steadiest option and the lowest ceiling.
  • Revenue share. The gym keeps an agreed cut of each session. Nothing to pay in a quiet week. Clients the gym finds for you usually come with a smaller share for you.
  • Rent or licence fee. A flat weekly or monthly fee and you keep everything you charge. The highest ceiling, but you pay it whether you train 5 clients or none.
Know which one you're signing

"Employed" and "contractor" get used loosely in this industry. A genuine employee gets super and leave. A contractor invoices under their own ABN and gets neither. The ATO has a free employee or contractor check at ato.gov.au if you're not sure.

Most trainers start employed, move to contracting once they have clients, and add online coaching later. Pick what suits you now.

Chapter 3

Set up the business

ABN

You need an Australian Business Number to invoice anyone directly. It's free from the Australian Business Register (abr.gov.au) and usually takes minutes. Most new trainers start as a sole trader: the simplest setup, though your business and personal money and liability aren't separate. Talk to an accountant about a company once you're hiring, opening a studio or earning more.

GST

You must register for GST once your GST turnover reaches A$75,000 a year (or you expect it to), within 21 days. Under that it's optional. Ask an accountant early if you're unsure.

Your paperwork

Have these before your first paid session:

  • Client agreement or terms: payment, your cancellation window, pausing, and refunds.
  • Health screening and waiver: the client tells you about relevant health matters and accepts the risks of exercise. It doesn't remove your duty of care, but insurers expect it.

Templates are fine to start from. Ask your insurer or a solicitor to look over the one you use.

Client information

You'll hold health details, contact details and maybe payment details. Keep them somewhere secure, not a shared spreadsheet, and tell clients what you collect and why. The Australian Privacy Principles apply to businesses over the small business threshold, and following them is good practice whatever your size.

Money

Open a separate bank account for the business before your first payment, and put a share of every payment aside for tax. Keep records for five years.

Chapter 4

Set your prices

New trainers most often charge too little, usually out of fear of losing clients before they have any. Work through these four steps in order.

  1. Start from your floor. Add up what a session costs you: the space (rent, gym fee or travel), insurance and registration, the tax you'll owe, and the unpaid hours on admin and marketing. That's the least you can charge.
  2. Then look at the local market. See what trainers with similar experience charge near you. Treat the cheapest prices as a warning, not a target.
  3. Sell packages, not single sessions. A block of 8 to 12 sessions, or a weekly or monthly plan, gives you steady income and gives clients a reason to keep going past week one.
  4. Raise prices for new clients first. Keep current clients on their rate for a while and start new ones at the higher price.
A quick check

If no one has ever paused at your price, it's probably too low.

Chapter 5

Find your first clients

Few trainers fill a diary from social media in the first month. The ones who fill up fastest do several of these at once.

  • Train a few people free, on purpose. If you're going solo there's no gym sending you walk-ins. A small number of free clients for a set number of weeks gives you real results, testimonials and referrals. Set the end date before you start.
  • Start with people who know you. Friends, family, past gym floor clients and teammates. A personal message asking if they, or someone they know, want to train works better than any ad when you're starting from zero.
  • Ask for referrals regularly. Ask clients when they're happiest with their progress, and make it a habit every 6 to 8 weeks.
  • Partner with local businesses. Physios, chiros and sports stores see people who need a trainer and don't compete with you. Refer to each other.
  • Post things only a coach would know. How you assess someone, why you chose an exercise, a real question a client asked. Specific posts build trust faster than more posts.
  • Make the first step easy. A short trial, like one session or a week of online check-ins, that feels like the real thing.

Chapter 6

The tools you need from day one

A calendar app, a notes app for programmes, payment links and a spreadsheet will get you through your first few clients. Past that, the time spent moving between them adds up.

Whatever you use, make sure you have a reliable way to do each of these:

  • Bookings: clients see your availability and book without a string of messages.
  • Payments: repeating payments for packages and plans, so you aren't chasing anyone.
  • Programmes: build them once, assign them, and change them easily.
  • Messages and check-ins: one thread per client.
  • Progress: weights, measurements and how they're going, so you both see the trend.

Common first year mistakes

Charging too littleIt's far harder to raise prices on a current client than to start a bit higher.
No agreement or waiverOne injury claim or payment dispute without paperwork can end a new business.
Marketing in burstsPosting for two weeks then going quiet. A steady pace you can keep works better.
Programmes from memoryFine for 3 clients, a mess well before 20.
Mixing personal and business moneyIt makes tax time hard and hides whether you're making money.
Saying yes to everyoneA client who isn't a good fit costs more in time and energy than they pay.

Chapter 8

Your launch checklist

Every step from this guide, in order.

  • Finish Certificate III and IV in Fitness with a registered provider
  • Register with AUSactive
  • Get public liability and professional indemnity insurance
  • Get current first aid and CPR
  • Choose how you'll work: employed, contractor, mobile, online or studio
  • Get an ABN and decide on sole trader or company
  • Check whether you need GST
  • Write your client agreement and terms
  • Set up a health screening and waiver form
  • Choose a secure place for client information
  • Open a separate business bank account
  • Work out your floor price, then check the local market
  • Decide on your packages or plans
  • Message your own network about your first clients
  • Make a referral ask part of your routine
  • Find 2 or 3 local businesses to refer with
  • Choose your booking, payment, programme and messaging tools
