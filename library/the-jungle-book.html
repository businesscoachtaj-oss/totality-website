The Jungle Book

Rudyard Kipling

PASSAGE 6 OF 177

Shere Khan had jumped at a woodcutter’s campfire, as Father Wolf had said, and was furious from the pain of his burned feet. But Father Wolf knew that the mouth of the cave was too narrow for a tiger to come in by. Even where he was, Shere Khan’s shoulders and forepaws were cramped for want of room, as a man’s would be if he tried to fight in a barrel.

“The Wolves are a free people,” said Father Wolf. “They take orders from the Head of the Pack, and not from any striped cattle-killer. The man’s cub is ours--to kill if we choose.”

“Ye choose and ye do not choose! What talk is this of choosing? By the bull that I killed, am I to stand nosing into your dog’s den for my fair dues? It is I, Shere Khan, who speak!”

The tiger’s roar filled the cave with thunder. Mother Wolf shook herself clear of the cubs and sprang forward, her eyes, like two green moons in the darkness, facing the blazing eyes of Shere Khan.

“And it is I, Raksha [The Demon], who answers. The man’s cub is mine, Lungri--mine to me! He shall not be killed. He shall live to run with the Pack and to hunt with the Pack; and in the end, look you, hunter of little naked cubs--frog-eater--fish-killer--he shall hunt thee! Now get hence, or by the Sambhur that I killed (I eat no starved cattle), back thou goest to thy mother, burned beast of the jungle, lamer than ever thou camest into the world! Go!”

What Tali kept

38 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

In The Jungle Book, Kipling shows Mother Wolf standing up to a tiger she fears, to protect a child that is not hers, so courage often means defending someone weaker.
In The Jungle Book, Baloo speaks up for the cub with no gift of words, showing that a plain truth from an ordinary voice can change the outcome in a room.
In The Jungle Book, Bagheera offers a fresh bull to settle the dispute, showing that a generous offer can end a quarrel that anger would only prolong.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli learns by paying close attention, so that every rustle, breath and bird call means something to him, which is how any craft is learned.
In The Jungle Book, Bagheera admits he was once caged by men and found his own strength, showing that an old hurt can become the source of a wise and loyal friend.
In The Jungle Book, Akela, an aging leader, offers his own life in place of the cub and shames the Pack for forgetting its honour, showing that a leader sometimes gives up what they hold.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli spares the Pack after they betray him, choosing mercy over revenge, which shows that restraint can be a stronger thing than anger.
In The Jungle Book, Baloo's maxims warn the proud young not to mistreat strangers and to stay humble, since the jungle is far larger than any one creature.
In The Jungle Book, Baloo teaches with firm, loving patience, arguing that a little discomfort now is better than harm later through ignorance.
In The Jungle Book, the Bandar-log flatter Mowgli with nuts and promises of leadership, showing how easy praise from those with no lasting loyalty can lure a person away from the friends who truly care.
In The Jungle Book, when Mowgli is captured he stops struggling and starts to think, sending word to his friends, which shows that a steady head is the first tool in a bad spot.
In The Jungle Book, Bagheera tells the panicking Baloo to sit still and make a plan, since chasing in a rush would only tire them out and cost them the trail.
In The Jungle Book, the monkeys copy the ways of men in a ruined city without understanding what anything is for, showing that imitating the look of a thing is not the same as knowing it.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli thanks Kaa courteously and promises to repay the debt one day, showing that gratitude is a debt a good person remembers and honours.
In The Jungle Book, Kaa's hypnotic dance leaves the monkeys unable to move by their own will, a warning that a smooth, clever voice can take away the judgement of even bold creatures.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli accepts his punishment without argument once he owns his mistake, and sleeps soundly afterwards, showing that admitting a wrong and paying for it clears the air.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli sees that to live among people he must learn their speech, so he copies every word he hears, showing that belonging begins with understanding.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli keeps his temper when children tease him, because the jungle taught him that losing control is costly, showing that self-restraint is a skill learned through hard lessons.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli challenges the village elders' tall tales, asking why anyone should trust ghost stories from a man who knows so little of the jungle at his own door.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli stops to think through his plan before acting, then asks for help when he cannot do it alone, showing that a clear head and honest asking beat rushing in.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli leaves the village without harming it because one woman had been kind to him, showing that a single act of kindness can hold back a person from revenge.
In The Jungle Book, Mowgli's song admits he belongs to two worlds and feels both pulls at once, showing that it is honest to hold mixed feelings rather than force them into one.
In The Jungle Book, the seal Sea Catch fights hard but never chases a beaten rival, because the Rules of the Beach forbid it, showing that a fair code keeps strength honourable.
In The White Seal, Kipling shows the young seal Kotick learning to swim through many small failures and retries, until he finds he truly belongs to the water, a picture of learning by doing.
In The White Seal, Kotick's friends tell him to accept the killing as part of the day's work, but he keeps asking questions, showing that refusing to accept what is simply normal is where change starts.
In The White Seal, a dying old seal tells Kotick to try once more, and Kotick keeps searching through years of disappointment, showing how a hope handed on can carry someone past their own discouragement.
In The White Seal, Kipling notes that the herd changes its ways only slowly, because creatures need a long time to turn things over in their minds, a useful thought for anyone trying to change a habit.
In Rikki-tikki-tavi, Kipling shows the cobra Nag making a grand speech about his marks while secretly afraid, a reminder that a loud threat often hides fear.
In Rikki-tikki-tavi, Rikki waits an hour, moving muscle by muscle, before choosing where to strike, showing that patient timing often counts for more than speed.
In Rikki-tikki-tavi, Darzee keeps singing his song of triumph while the real danger is still close, showing how easy it is to celebrate before the job is finished.
In Rikki-tikki-tavi, Rikki is proud of his victory but does not grow too proud, and keeps watch over his garden, showing that a real win is kept by steady vigilance rather than boasting.
In Toomai of the Elephants, Petersen Sahib notices the courage of a small boy who joined a wild elephant drive, and says he may grow into a hunter, showing that a good leader sees effort even when others do not.
In Toomai of the Elephants, a driver warns that a man who lives in one hut knows only its four walls, a reminder that a narrow life can leave us blind to the wider world.
In Toomai of the Elephants, Little Toomai, alone in the dark among hundreds of elephants, sets his teeth and stays still, showing that steady nerve in a frightening moment is a quiet form of courage.
In Toomai of the Elephants, Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa follow the tracks to check the child's strange story, and only believe him once the evidence is in, showing that good judgement tests a claim before accepting or dismissing it.
In Her Majesty's Servants, an old troop-horse says it is forgivable to be scared of things one does not understand, and that getting cross with others for their fear is the worse fault.
In Her Majesty's Servants, the elephant Two Tails admits he knows just enough to be uncomfortable and not enough to carry on regardless, a candid picture of how foresight can make fear heavier.
In Her Majesty's Servants, a young mule asks why they must fight at all and is told to obey and ask no questions, and the scene leaves the question hanging, a reminder that asking why is still worth doing.
