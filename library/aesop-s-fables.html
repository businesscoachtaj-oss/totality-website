Aesop's Fables

Aesop

PASSAGE 3 OF 108

On the invitation of Croesus he fixed his residence at Sardis, and was employed by that monarch in various difficult and delicate affairs of state. In his discharge of these commissions he visited the different petty republics of Greece. At one time he is found in Corinth, and at another in Athens, endeavoring, by the narration of some of his wise fables, to reconcile the inhabitants of those cities to the administration of their respective rulers, Pariander and Pisistratus. One of these ambassadorial missions, undertaken at the command of Croesus, was the occasion of his death. Having been sent to Delphi with a large sum of gold for distribution among the citizens, he was so provoked at their covetousness that he refused to divide the money, and sent it back to his master. The Delphians, enraged at this treatment, accused him of impiety, and, in spite of his sacred character as ambassador, executed him as a public criminal. This cruel death of Æsop was not unavenged. The citizens of Delphi were visited with a series of calamities, until they made a public reparation of their crime; and "The blood of Æsop" became a well-known adage, bearing witness to the truth that deeds of wrong would not pass unpunished. Neither did the great fabulist lack posthumous honors; for a statue was erected to his memory at Athens, the work of Lysippus, one of the most famous of Greek sculptors. Phædrus thus immortalizes the event:--

Æsopo ingentem statuam posuere Attici,
Servumque collocarunt æterna in basi:
Patere honoris scirent ut cuncti viam;
Nec generi tribui sed virtuti gloriam.

What Tali kept

112 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

In Aesop's life, his fables were a tool for persuasion. He used them to win over the people of Corinth and Athens, a reminder that a good story can carry a point further than a lecture.
In The Stag at the Pool, Aesop shows a stag so proud of his antlers that he despises the legs that would have saved him. What is truly valuable is often underrated.
In The Wolf and the Lamb, Aesop shows that a tyrant will always find a pretext, so the innocent cannot reason their way out of an unjust oppressor.
In The Bear and the Fox, Aesop reminds us to show people respect while they are alive, not only once they are gone.
In The One-Eyed Doe, Aesop shows that danger often comes from the direction we least suspect, so we should not guard only the side we fear.
In The Mouse, the Frog, and the Hawk, Aesop shows that whoever plots harm for others is often caught by their own scheme.
In The Lion and the Boar, Aesop shows that rivals who keep fighting are watched by others who will profit from their defeat, so making peace can be the wiser choice.
In The Mischievous Dog, Aesop shows that people who win notoriety often mistake it for fame, when the bell and clog they wear mark them out as a nuisance.
In The Wolf and the Sheep, Aesop shows that a hypocrite's fine words are easily seen through, since the wolf asks for help while planning to eat the helper.
In The Widow and her Little Maidens, Aesop shows that an unlawful act taken to escape trouble only brings more trouble.
In The Fox and the Lion, Aesop shows that familiarity softens fear, since the fox grew bolder with each meeting.
In The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse, Aesop shows that a modest meal in safety is better than a rich feast surrounded by danger.
In The Monkey and the Dolphin, Aesop shows that a falsehood needs more lies to hold it up, and sooner or later it will get the teller into trouble.
In The Trumpeter taken Prisoner, Aesop shows that the one who stirs others to fight carries as much blame as those who fight.
In The Boy and the Nettle, Aesop shows that a timid touch invites a sting, while a firm grip makes a hard task easier.
In The Lion and the Mouse, Aesop shows that ambition can push us to reach for more than we can hold, and alliances built on that pride often end in ruin.
In The Milkmaid and her Pot of Milk, Aesop shows how quickly plans built on imagined profits can collapse, so it pays to wait for real gains before spending them in your head.
In The Man and the Satyr, Aesop shows that someone who blows hot and cold with the same breath cannot be trusted by anyone.
In The Mother and the Wolf, Aesop shows that words spoken in anger or thoughtlessness are not always meant, so it pays to wait before believing them.
In The Old Man and the Three Young Men, Aesop shows that planting for those who come after us is a duty, and that none of us can be sure of tomorrow.
In The Horse and the Stag, Aesop shows that whoever seeks to injure others often injures only himself, since the horse handed himself over to man to punish a stag.
In The Lion and the Dolphin, Aesop shows that each of us does best in our own element, and blaming others for what nature denied us helps no one.
In The Fighting Cocks and the Eagle, Aesop shows that pride goes before destruction, because the cock that crowed loudest from the high wall was the one the eagle spotted and carried off.
In a story of a shepherd who left his flock in a wolf's care, Aesop shows that an evil mind will show itself in evil action, sooner or later.
In The Frog and the Ox, Aesop shows the folly of puffing ourselves up to match something far bigger than us, because the frog could never swell to the size of the ox.
In The Lion and the Mouse, Aesop shows that no one is too weak to do good, since the mouse who was spared later freed the lion from the hunters' ropes.
In The Old Hound, Aesop shows that an old dog should be judged for what he was in his strength and not blamed for infirmities that age brings.
In The Crow and the Pitcher, Aesop shows that when we are stuck, patient effort and clever thinking can solve what brute strength cannot. Necessity is the mother of invention.
In The Ass Eating Thistles, Aesop shows that what one person rejects another may value highly, so we should not judge others by our own tastes.
In The Trees and the Axe, Aesop shows that giving up the rights of others can put our own at risk, because the first concession starts a loss that spreads.
In The King's Son and the Painted Lion, Aesop shows that hiding from a dreaded fate can bring about the very harm we fear.
In a story of travellers who waited for a ship that turned out to be a bundle of sticks, Aesop shows how our expectations can run ahead of what life delivers.
In The Rat and the Elephant, Aesop shows that sharing one trait with the great does not make us their equal in everything.
In The Eagle and the Kite, Aesop shows that a smooth promise made to win something is worth little, since the kite promised an ostrich and brought back a mouse.
In The Fisherman and the Little Fish, Aesop shows that a sure gain in hand is worth more than a bigger profit that is only promised for later.
In The Fox and the Crow, Aesop shows that flattery serves the one who gives it, so a wise listener is wary of praise that seems to want something in return.
In The Mountain in Labor, Aesop shows that a great deal of noise can end in a very small result, so judge the outcome rather than the commotion.
In a story of two travellers and a bear, Aesop shows that misfortune is what tests the sincerity of friends.
In The Two Soldiers and the Robber, Aesop shows that a boast from someone who ran at the first sign of danger earns no trust. A record of action counts for more than words.
In The Two Frogs, Aesop shows the value of thinking through consequences before acting, since the frog who asked how they would climb back out asked the wiser question.
In The Mouse and the Boasting Rat, Aesop shows that a loud boast of courage is no defence, and a plan built on bluster can leave others in the lurch.
In The Thief and the House-Dog, Aesop shows that sudden kindness from someone with hidden aims should make us more watchful, not less.
In The Fowler and the Ringdove, Aesop shows that people often fall into the very trap they set for others, as the hunter was stung by the adder while aiming at a bird.
In The North Wind and the Sun, Aesop shows that gentle warmth persuades where force fails, because the traveller shed his cloak only when the sun shone on him.
In The Snake and the Cottager, Aesop shows that it is hard to forget an injury while the one who caused it stands in front of us every day.
In The Boasting Traveler, Aesop shows that the best cure for a boaster is to put his claims to the test, and let the facts speak for themselves.
In The Stag and the Fawn, Aesop shows that the loudest braggarts are often the most fearful when real danger arrives.
In The Hare and the Hound, Aesop shows that a runner fighting for its life will often outlast one running only for a meal, because the stakes shape how hard we push.
In The Dog in the Manger, Aesop shows that we should not deny others a blessing just because we cannot enjoy it ourselves.
In The Cat and the Fox, Aesop shows that a little common sense is often worth more than a great deal of cunning.
In The Frogs Asking for a King, Aesop shows that before we try to change our condition, we should be sure the change will leave us better off.
In The Dog and his Master's Dinner, Aesop shows that someone who stops to argue with temptation is very likely to give in to it.
In The Buffoon and the Countryman, Aesop shows that critics are not always to be relied on, since the crowd cheered the fake performance and missed the real one.
In The Fox and the Goat, Aesop shows that we should look before we leap, since the fox had never checked the way out before he went down the well.
In The Oxen and the Butchers, Aesop shows that we should not rush to replace one evil with another, because the old butcher knew the known harm was better than the unknown one.
In The Lark and her Young Ones, Aesop shows that when the field owner stops relying on friends and does the work himself, it is time to act. Self-help is the best help.
In The Boy and the Filberts, Aesop shows that grabbing too much at once leaves us stuck, and taking half is the way to get anything out.
In The Woman and her Hen, Aesop shows that greed defeats itself, since the woman who overfed her hen for two eggs a day ended with no eggs at all.
In The Hawk and the Nightingale, Aesop shows that a sure gain in hand beats chasing bigger prizes that are not yet even in sight.
In The Hen and the Swallow, Aesop shows that nourishing something harmful brings it to life, and sooner or later it turns on the one who fed it.
