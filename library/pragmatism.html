Pragmatism

William James

217 passages98 things kept

PASSAGE 6 OF 217

I think with Mr. Chesterton in this matter. I know that you, ladies and gentlemen, have a philosophy, each and all of you, and that the most interesting and important thing about you is the way in which it determines the perspective in your several worlds. You know the same of me. And yet I confess to a certain tremor at the audacity of the enterprise which I am about to begin. For the philosophy which is so important in each of us is not a technical matter; it is our more or less dumb sense of what life honestly and deeply means. It is only partly got from books; it is our individual way of just seeing and feeling the total push and pressure of the cosmos. I have no right to assume that many of you are students of the cosmos in the class-room sense, yet here I stand desirous of interesting you in a philosophy which to no small extent has to be technically treated. I wish to fill you with sympathy with a contemporaneous tendency in which I profoundly believe, and yet I have to talk like a professor to you who are not students. Whatever universe a professor believes in must at any rate be a universe that lends itself to lengthy discourse. A universe definable in two sentences is something for which the professorial intellect has no use. No faith in anything of that cheap kind! I have heard friends and colleagues try to popularize philosophy in this very hall, but they soon grew dry, and then technical, and the results were only partially encouraging. So my enterprise is a bold one. The founder of pragmatism himself recently gave a course of lectures at the Lowell Institute with that very word in its title-flashes of brilliant light relieved against Cimmerian darkness! None of us, I fancy, understood ALL that he said--yet here I stand, making a very similar venture.

98 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

William James held that everyone has a philosophy, a half-spoken sense of what life honestly means, and that it quietly shapes how each person sees their world.
William James said philosophy does not bake bread, yet it can give people courage and light up the wider view of the world.
William James noticed that every philosopher's temperament quietly biases their conclusions, so honesty means naming that bias rather than hiding it behind neutral reasons.
William James observed that tender-minded and tough-minded people tend to look down on each other, each judging the other as lacking in some way.
William James warned that a good intellectual conscience cannot survive mixing incompatible beliefs, even though most of us like a bit of both sides.
William James argued that a philosophy so abstract it never touches concrete life is sterile, because it could fit almost any world and so tells us nothing about this one.
William James described abstract philosophy as a clean marble temple that can become an escape from messy real life rather than an honest account of it.
William James held that the actual world is wide open, while tidy systems must close themselves off, so a neat system can miss what is really happening.
William James argued that the thoughtful amateur, who asks philosophy for a way to live, is a fair judge of it, and any theory that leaves actual things blank will fail that test.
William James suggested that every theory is in part a place of escape from raw reality, and that abstract ideas can still give the mind a worthwhile home if they stay tied to facts.
William James's pragmatic method settles a dispute by asking what practical difference each side would make. If no difference can be traced, the argument is idle, and a serious dispute should always show one.
Charles Peirce held that the meaning of a thought is the practical conduct it could produce, so a vague idea becomes clear once you ask what it would actually do.
William James argued that any difference in abstract truth should show up as a difference in real life, and that philosophy is worth doing only if it shows what difference an idea makes to you.
William James warned that a grand word such as God, Reason or the Absolute can feel like the end of a search, but under the pragmatic test it should become a plan for work that changes things.
William James likened pragmatism to a corridor in a hotel. It has no dogmas of its own, only a method, and every kind of thinker must pass through it to get in or out of their room.
William James noted that scientific laws are a man-made shorthand for summarising facts and leading to new ones. They are useful tools rather than perfect copies of reality.
William James described how people change their minds. They keep as much of their old beliefs as they can, changing one thing at a time until a new idea fits the existing stock with the least disturbance.
William James noticed that when people meet surprising facts they usually either ignore them or attack the people who report them, because loyalty to older truths comes first for most of us.
William James argued that concreteness is a real virtue. A thinker who prefers a pale, pure outline to the rich thicket of everyday reality is often mistaking thinness for nobility.
William James held that a view which is noble but unfit for humble, everyday service should count against it, because real life is mostly sweat and dirt.
William James judged an idea of the Absolute by what it does for people, such as letting them take a moral holiday from anxiety and trust that the world is in good hands, and not by its abstract grandeur alone.
William James saw truth as one kind of good: a belief counts as true when it proves good to hold, for definite and traceable reasons, such as helping us live well.
William James observed that the greatest threat to any one of our beliefs is often our other beliefs, which clash with it and cost us something we value.
William James held that a good test of an idea is how well it fits every part of life, and that a method this open should count even the humblest and most personal experience.
William James praised Berkeley for testing an idea by its practical difference. Matter means the sensations it brings us, so if a notion makes no difference to experience, it adds nothing.
William James noted that Locke grounded personal identity in memory and conscious continuity, and that fairness depends on this, since no one should answer for what they cannot remember.
William James pointed out that many arguments between spirit and matter are really just taste, with one side calling matter gross and the other calling spirit pure, and that such preferences are not evidence.
William James argued that a philosophical debate with no traceable future consequences is idle, and that every genuine debate should involve some practical issue, however distant or uncertain.
William James held that the real objection to materialism is not that matter is crude, but that it offers no lasting guarantee for our highest hopes and ideals.
William James observed that many people deeply need to believe their ideals will not be finally lost, and he saw that this need can shape how a whole philosophy feels.
William James said an abstract word like design is a blank cartridge: it carries no consequences until you ask what sort of design and what sort of designer, and answer that from the facts.
William James held that the only possibility we can rationally claim is that things may become better, so a hopeful stance toward the future is a reasonable one.
William James argued that the vital question is what this world is going to become, and that philosophy should turn its attention from lofty first principles towards the facts and the future.
William James compared abstract ideas to air above water: not enough to live on alone, but able to redirect how we swim through experience, so something not sufficient for life can still shape it.
William James held that our minds aim at totality, which needs both the variety of things and their unity. Curiosity about particular details runs alongside the urge to systematise, and both are part of a good mind.
William James noticed that the vague feeling that everything is one can dazzle a thinker into losing interest in the details, whereas a more empirical mind keeps its curiosity about specific facts.
William James saw oneness and manyness as equally real and co-ordinate. Sometimes we need conductors that join things, sometimes non-conductors that separate them, and wisdom lies in knowing which the moment calls for.
William James pointed out that learning from the past depends on things coming in kinds. If every fact were unique, we could not reason from past experience to future experience at all.
William James observed that people start with vague goals such as being great or good, and that each step reveals new chances and closes others, so purposes must be reshaped as we go.
William James held that it is rash to call the world one story or one purpose dogmatically. Unity is a legitimate hypothesis, but it needs better evidence than we have before we treat it as settled.
William James treats the idea of one all-knowing Absolute as a hypothesis to test, not a truth to accept just because it sounds grand.
William James's test for any grand idea is what practical difference it would make to live by it. That keeps you grounded instead of carried away by excitement.
William James notices that a strong feeling can shield a weak argument from criticism, so it is worth checking the feeling behind a conclusion you are sure of.
William James suggests the unity of the world may grow over time through human effort, rather than being fixed from the start.
William James says pragmatism loosens rigid theories, so a belief held with fierce certainty can be tested again and softened by reasonable discussion.
William James's working question for any idea is how far experience verifies it, so a good pragmatist turns towards the facts rather than abstract certainty.
William James observes that our knowledge grows in spots, so new learning changes older beliefs gradually, as we line up old opinions with new ones.
William James says we tend to patch our beliefs rather than renew them, so new facts usually soak in and are tinged by what we already think.
William James argues that our most basic ways of thinking are very old and survive every later change of opinion, so it is worth examining them.
William James says all our concepts are tools for handling facts by thinking about them, and experience does not come labelled until we work out what it is.
James compares raw experience to Boston weather: chaotic until we place each event within a larger pattern, and that placing is what makes it understood.
James quotes Santayana saying that understanding the order of events makes each moment meaningful, so calm times fill with power and hard times with resources.
William James says kinds and sameness of kind are the instruments of logic, and without them our experience would have no order to find our way by.
James suggests our search for causes began with asking who or what is to blame for a problem, a habit worth noticing when we look for reasons behind trouble.
William James notes that everyday concepts work well for practical purposes but blur at the edges when we speculate, so it pays to know where they stop applying.
William James observes that ideas which look purely theoretical often pay off in practical results nobody could have foreseen at the start.
James warns that being established or fashionable is not proof of truth. Common sense is entrenched mainly because it came first.
James reports that scientists treat their theoretical entities as useful short-cuts, like logarithms, and advises not to be fooled into taking them literally.
James holds that different ways of thinking suit different parts of life, so judge them by their use rather than by a claim to absolute truth.
William James argues our theories are instruments for adapting to reality, not final revelations, which is why each can be useful for some purposes and none settles the matter for all.
