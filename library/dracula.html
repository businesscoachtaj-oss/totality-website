Dracula

Chapter I

Jonathan Harker’s Journal

(Kept in shorthand.)

3 May. Bistritz.--Left Munich at 8:35 P. M., on 1st May, arriving at Vienna early next morning; should have arrived at 6:46, but train was an hour late. Buda-Pesth seems a wonderful place, from the glimpse which I got of it from the train and the little I could walk through the streets. I feared to go very far from the station, as we had arrived late and would start as near the correct time as possible. The impression I had was that we were leaving the West and entering the East; the most western of splendid bridges over the Danube, which is here of noble width and depth, took us among the traditions of Turkish rule.

We left in pretty good time, and came after nightfall to Klausenburgh. Here I stopped for the night at the Hotel Royale. I had for dinner, or rather supper, a chicken done up some way with red pepper, which was very good but thirsty. (Mem., get recipe for Mina.) I asked the waiter, and he said it was called “paprika hendl,” and that, as it was a national dish, I should be able to get it anywhere along the Carpathians. I found my smattering of German very useful here; indeed, I don’t know how I should be able to get on without it.

In Dracula, the Count's warm letter of welcome makes Harker feel expected and at ease, showing how a little courtesy can disarm even a careful person.
In Dracula, Harker brushes aside the frightened innkeeper's warnings and presses on, a reminder that ignoring an honest warning from someone who knows the place can be costly.
In Dracula, Harker accepts a crucifix from the old woman out of courtesy, even against what he was taught, showing how kindness can open a person to things they would normally refuse.
In Dracula, Harker is touched by the crowd that fears for him, showing that people who look strange to us are often acting out of care.
In Dracula, Harker does not dare to question the driver because he feels he has no power to change anything, showing how a person who feels helpless often stays silent when they most need to ask.
In Dracula, Harker notes that only a person who faces a danger directly can understand what it really means, a reminder that some knowledge can only come from experience.
In Dracula, the warmth and courtesy of the host dissolve Harker's doubts, showing how comfort can quiet the fears that good sense would otherwise raise.
