Peter Pan

J. M. Barrie

PASSAGE 5 OF 158

Mrs. Darling loved to have everything just so, and Mr. Darling had a passion for being exactly like his neighbours; so, of course, they had a nurse. As they were poor, owing to the amount of milk the children drank, this nurse was a prim Newfoundland dog, called Nana, who had belonged to no one in particular until the Darlings engaged her. She had always thought children important, however, and the Darlings had become acquainted with her in Kensington Gardens, where she spent most of her spare time peeping into perambulators, and was much hated by careless nursemaids, whom she followed to their homes and complained of to their mistresses. She proved to be quite a treasure of a nurse. How thorough she was at bath-time, and up at any moment of the night if one of her charges made the slightest cry. Of course her kennel was in the nursery. She had a genius for knowing when a cough is a thing to have no patience with and when it needs stocking around your throat. She believed to her last day in old-fashioned remedies like rhubarb leaf, and made sounds of contempt over all this new-fangled talk about germs, and so on. It was a lesson in propriety to see her escorting the children to school, walking sedately by their side when they were well behaved, and butting them back into line if they strayed. On John’s footer days she never once forgot his sweater, and she usually carried an umbrella in her mouth in case of rain. There is a room in the basement of Miss Fulsom’s school where the nurses wait. They sat on forms, while Nana lay on the floor, but that was the only difference. They affected to ignore her as of an inferior social status to themselves, and she despised their light talk. She resented visits to the nursery from Mrs. Darling’s friends, but if they did come she first whipped off Michael’s pinafore and put him into the one with blue braiding, and smoothed out Wendy and made a dash at John’s hair.

What Tali kept

41 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

In Peter Pan, J. M. Barrie suggests children grow up the moment they realise they will, and Wendy learns it from one look at her mother.
In Peter Pan, Barrie makes Nana, a dog who works as a nurse, the one who takes the children most seriously, showing that real care is shown through attention rather than status.
In Peter Pan, Barrie pictures a mother tidying her children's minds each night, folding the day's naughtiness away and setting out the prettier thoughts for morning. It reads like a fair description of an evening review.
Barrie notes that Mrs. Darling believed in Peter as a child, but grown-up good sense made her doubt him. The story quietly warns that sensible adults can lose their wonder without noticing.
In Peter Pan, the Darlings sit for years replaying every "if only", and Barrie shows how dwelling on what went wrong can keep a family stuck instead of moving on.
In Peter Pan, Mrs. Darling stays calm when her husband rages over a tie and simply offers to help. Barrie shows that a steady, kind response can defuse a storm faster than an argument.
In Peter Pan, Mr. Darling knows he is behaving badly but cannot back down, because his pride will not let him admit a mistake. Barrie shows how wounded pride can keep a person pushing on when they already know better.
In Peter Pan, Wendy meets a crying stranger with courtesy and curiosity rather than alarm. A polite, open question is how a friendship begins.
In Peter Pan, Peter says a fairy dies every time a child stops believing in fairies. Barrie uses it to show that wonder survives only while someone keeps believing in it.
In Peter Pan, Peter wins Wendy over with praise and promises that she would be respected and needed. Barrie shows how flattery can talk a person into leaving the safety of home.
In Peter Pan, Peter rescues Michael mostly to show off his cleverness, and the story notes that this is not the same as caring. Kindness done for applause is a thin kind of kindness.
In Peter Pan, Peter forgets people quickly, so Wendy has to keep saying her own name to be remembered. Barrie shows that being known often takes patient, repeated reminders.
In Peter Pan, Barrie observes that a jealous heart has room for only one feeling at a time, so Tink can't be kind to Wendy while she is envious. It is a sharp way of showing how envy crowds out goodwill.
In Peter Pan, Barrie says Tootles's bad luck gave him a gentle sadness, but it sweetened his nature rather than souring it. Hard knocks do not have to make a person bitter.
In Peter Pan, Barrie says Captain Hook is never more sinister than when he is most polite. Good manners can hide cruelty, so a polished surface is no proof of a good character.
In Peter Pan, Peter raises the arrow to strike Tootles twice and cannot bring it down, because he sees Wendy is alive. Barrie shows how a moment's pause, and a fact checked, can stop a hasty judgment.
In Peter Pan, Barrie notes that Peter treats make-believe and truth as the same thing, while the other boys know the difference. The story suggests that taking play seriously is a kind of imagination worth keeping.
In Peter Pan, Wendy worries her brothers are forgetting their parents, so she sets them quizzes about home. Barrie shows that keeping loved ones in mind takes deliberate effort, not just time.
In Peter Pan, Wendy keeps the boys on a cold rock because of her own bedtime rule, and it nearly costs them. Barrie shows that a rule followed too rigidly can work against the very people it is meant to protect.
In Peter Pan, Barrie notes that Hook needed his own belief in himself more than his men's belief in him. Once the inner confidence goes, outside respect tends to follow.
In Peter Pan, Peter could have finished Hook when he had the higher ground, but judges that unfair and gives his enemy a hand up. Barrie shows that fairness is a choice a person makes, even in a fight.
In Peter Pan, Barrie says every child is shaken the first time they are treated unfairly, and is never quite the same boy again. It is a reminder that people carry the first sting of unfairness for a long time.
In Peter Pan, the Never bird gives up her nest to rescue Peter, though the two cannot understand each other's words. Barrie shows that generosity needs no shared language.
In Peter Pan, the children enjoy one last glad hour without knowing what is coming. Barrie makes the reader feel how precious an ordinary evening is while it lasts.
In Peter Pan, Barrie observes that children can be careless with the people who love them because they trust that love will always be there. It is a gentle warning that taking care for granted is its own kind of blindness.
In Peter Pan, Tootles, usually overlooked, rises to the trust Wendy places in him and speaks with dignity. Barrie shows that people often grow to match the faith others put in them.
In Peter Pan, Peter has thought his mother out and remembers only her bad points, so he no longer misses her. Barrie shows how holding on to a grievance can quietly reshape what we remember about the people we have lost.
In Peter Pan, Barrie suggests Hook's hatred of Peter comes from Peter's easy confidence, which unsettles him like a sparrow in a lion's cage. Resentment of someone else's ease can grow into an obsession.
In Peter Pan, Peter pretends not to care that Wendy has gone, playing his pipes and sleeping in a huff. Barrie shows that bravado is often how hurt hides itself.
In Peter Pan, Barrie says Hook is moved by the peaceful sight of his sleeping enemy, but Peter's smug look steels his heart. Irritation can drown out a person's better nature.
In Peter Pan, Tinker Bell, who has been unkind to Peter's friends, drinks the poison meant for him. Barrie shows that someone who has behaved badly can still act selflessly when it matters most.
In Peter Pan, Hook cannot understand why the children love the plain-hearted Smee and not himself. Barrie hints that kindness which seeks no admiration is the kind people trust most.
In Peter Pan, John and Michael refuse to swear an oath they do not believe, even knowing the plank waits. Barrie shows that courage can be quiet, just a refusal to betray what you hold to be true.
In Peter Pan, Barrie notes that Peter turns the crocodile's misfortune to his own use without a thought for how it feels. The passage is a quiet prompt to ask who a situation is costing before you profit from it.
In Peter Pan, Hook dies clinging to outward appearances, his tie and socks all right, even as his inner life falls apart. Barrie shows how a person can guard a reputation so hard that they lose sight of what matters.
In Peter Pan, Mr. Darling answers his mistake by sleeping in a kennel and courteously lifting his hat to passers-by. Barrie suggests that owning a failure openly, without hiding behind it, can win a quiet kind of respect.
In Peter Pan, Wendy wonders whether she and her brothers remember the old life as well as they thought. Barrie reminds us that memories of home can be less reliable than we assume.
In Peter Pan, Mrs. Darling has dreamed of her children so often that she cannot believe they are really there. Barrie shows how longing can make the real thing hard to recognise when it finally arrives.
In Peter Pan, Mr. Darling is hurt to feel treated as a cypher in his own home, and the boys reassure him. Barrie suggests that everyone wants to be consulted about the place they live.
In Peter Pan, the boys lose the power to fly after they start school. Barrie says they called it lack of practice, but it really meant they had stopped believing, which is a sharp observation about how wonder fades.
In Peter Pan, Barrie ends with Wendy, now grown, watching her daughter fly off with Peter through wet-eyed smiles. Letting children go can itself be a loving act.
