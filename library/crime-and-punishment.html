A few months later Dostoevsky died. He was followed to the grave by a vast multitude of mourners, who “gave the hapless man the funeral of a king.” He is still probably the most widely read writer in Russia.

In the words of a Russian critic, who seeks to explain the feeling inspired by Dostoevsky: “He was one of ourselves, a man of our blood and our bone, but one who has suffered and has seen so much more deeply than we have his insight impresses us as wisdom... that wisdom of the heart which we seek that we may learn from it how to live. All his other gifts came to him from nature, this he won for himself and through it he became great.”

Crime and Punishment

Part I
Chapter I

On an exceptionally hot evening early in July a young man came out of the garret in which he lodged in S. Place and walked slowly, as though in hesitation, towards K. bridge.

He had successfully avoided meeting his landlady on the staircase. His garret was under the roof of a high, five-storied house and was more like a cupboard than a room. The landlady who provided him with garret, dinners, and attendance, lived on the floor below, and every time he went out he was obliged to pass her kitchen, the door of which invariably stood open. And each time he passed, the young man had a sick, frightened feeling, which made him scowl and feel ashamed. He was hopelessly in debt to his landlady, and was afraid of meeting her.

159 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

Dostoevsky is remembered for a wisdom of the heart, won through his own suffering, that readers say teaches them how to live.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov fears taking a new step more than anything, and talks so much about a bold idea that he never acts on it.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov finds that a small, overlooked detail can spoil a whole plan, so the trivial things need as much care as the big ones.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov asks himself what he would do if he is this frightened before a bold act, a fair test that Dostoevsky uses to show how fear grows as an idea gets closer.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov is horrified by his own dark thoughts and runs off to a tavern to escape them, a reminder that we often flee an uncomfortable thought rather than look at it.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoevsky notes that some strangers draw us in from the first moment, before a word is spoken, and that we often read meaning into those first impressions.
In Crime and Punishment, the drunken clerk Marmeladov says every person needs at least one place where people feel for him, a simple truth about why belonging matters.
In Crime and Punishment, Sonia gives what she has to a family in need without a word and without looking at anyone, showing that the deepest kindness is often silent.
In Crime and Punishment, Marmeladov's family lifts him with trust after he finds work, and he says that treatment changes how he feels about himself, showing how much a man can rise when people expect good of him.
In Crime and Punishment, Marmeladov says being forgiven without blame can hurt more than blame, because it asks more of the one who was forgiven.
In Crime and Punishment, Marmeladov argues that mercy goes most readily to those who never thought they deserved it, a reminder that humility and a clear view of our own faults can open the door to kindness.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov decides that people are likely to be scoundrels, a bleak theory that sounds clever to him but hollows out how he treats everyone around him.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov pulls away from everyone like a tortoise in its shell, showing how isolation can feel comfortable while it quietly deepens a person's troubles.
In Crime and Punishment, Marfa Petrovna, who wronged Dounia, publicly clears her name and begs forgiveness, showing that owning a mistake openly is a real way to put it right.
In Crime and Punishment, a mother warns her son not to judge a man too hastily, since first impressions and prejudices are hard to correct once they set in.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov reads every kind word from his family as a trick, showing how resentment can make even love look like deception.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov judges a match by its tone rather than its numbers, noticing that how people treat each other early on tends to become the pattern later.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov notes that people with generous hearts often refuse to see the truth until it forces itself on them, turning a plain goose into a swan to avoid it.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoevsky shows how someone who sacrifices for a loved one can talk themselves into calling it duty, and how a good cause can hide a bad bargain.
In Crime and Punishment, an idea that starts as a daydream slowly takes a menacing shape, a reminder to notice when a fantasy is quietly becoming a plan.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov steps in to protect a girl, then in an instant turns his back on her, showing how quickly feelings swing and how a good impulse can be dropped as fast as it was taken up.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoevsky shows how a comforting phrase like "percentage" lets people stop feeling responsible for real harm to real people, so watch for the words that make a hard thing easy to ignore.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin keeps his good humour through poverty and setbacks, showing that steady warmth can be the most useful kind of friend.
In Crime and Punishment, a child's dream shows a crowd laughing as a helpless horse is beaten, and only the child sees clearly what is wrong, a lesson on how a group can make cruelty feel like a joke.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov finds his reasoning "true as arithmetic", yet his own heart refuses to act on it, a reminder that a clever argument can still fail the test of conscience.
In Crime and Punishment, a student argues that one wicked act could pay for many good deeds, until his friend asks whether he would do it himself, a sharp test for any argument that makes harm sound like simple arithmetic.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov ends up hunting for arguments as if something outside him were pushing him on, and the doubt he keeps hiding is the real signal.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoevsky suggests that a clear view of the consequences would have stopped Raskolnikov, since the horror he feels is his own conscience speaking, a reminder to listen to that voice before acting.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov's torment begins inside him, with a mind that cannot rest, long before any outside authority catches up, a picture of how guilt punishes the person who carries it.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov, weighed down by fear, feels a sudden urge to confess everything, a sign that the truth keeps pressing for an exit even when we are busy hiding from it.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov trembles with relief when his summons turns out to be about a small debt, not his crime, a sign of how fear can attach itself to the wrong thing.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky shows a desperate man pouring his private troubles out to officials who have no interest in them, and feeling more exposed for it.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky shows how shame and vanity can drop away in an instant, leaving a man feeling completely alone.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky shows a plan settled on in a fever losing its certainty in daylight, so that a man who once felt sure ends up wandering and unable to act.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky shows a man blaming his illness for doubts he would rather not face, when the real question is why he acted at all.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov turns away the one friend who could help him, insisting he needs nothing from anyone. Dostoyevsky shows how guilt and pride can cut a person off from help.
In Crime and Punishment, a beaten and ragged man is pressed for coins by a poor mother and daughter who only see that he is hurt. Dostoyevsky shows kindness coming from unexpected people.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin looks after his sick friend with plain, unfussy care, simply turning up with soup and tea and staying close, which is often what real help looks like.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin quietly takes on his friend's debt and simply hands back the proof. Dostoyevsky shows that real help can be hard to accept when pride is involved.
In Crime and Punishment, a man who has hidden something keeps going back to the hiding place, unable to settle. Dostoyevsky shows guilt as a restless loop that gives no peace.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin argues that facts are not everything, since much of the work lies in how you interpret them. It is a useful reminder for anyone weighing evidence.
In Crime and Punishment, a pawnbroker's shrewd maxim is that the further you hide a thing, the quicker it will be found. Dostoyevsky shows how concealment so often gives itself away.
In Crime and Punishment, a doctor warns his friend that a story which fits too neatly, with every detail falling into place, should make you suspicious. Dostoyevsky shows how a tidy explanation can be a trap.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov suspects a visitor of reciting fashionable opinions he has learnt by heart to look clever. Dostoyevsky shows how borrowed ideas can pass for wisdom.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin cuts through a man's fine phrases about progress to see what sort of man he really is. Dostoyevsky shows that fluent talk can hide a shallow character.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin suggests that a society which has grown used to having everything handed over is left floundering when the hard hour comes. It is a useful thought about dependence and character.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov feels a sudden calm once he has fixed on a desperate course. Dostoyevsky shows that such calm comes from a settled purpose, not from being right.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov recalls a condemned man's thought that even a narrow ledge is better than dying. Dostoyevsky shows how fiercely people cling to life, however bleak it gets.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov points out that a scheme relying on many untrustworthy partners tends to fall apart through one careless slip. It is a fair warning for any business that depends on people who cannot be relied on.
In Crime and Punishment, Zametov warns that even a practised, desperate man cannot always rely on his own nerve when it comes to the test. Dostoyevsky shows how hard it is to know yourself under pressure.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin tells his friend, who has just pushed him away, that people who quarrel with those who care for them often come back. Dostoyevsky shows loyalty that keeps knocking even when it is turned away.
In Crime and Punishment, a policeman throws off his coat and boots and jumps into a canal to pull a drowning woman out, without any fuss. Dostoyevsky shows courage in ordinary people doing their duty.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov, lost in his own torment, still insists that an injured stranger be carried home at once and offers to pay for it. Dostoyevsky shows that compassion can break through even the most troubled heart.
In Crime and Punishment, Katerina Ivanovna would rather wear herself out washing linen at night than let dirt into her home, even while poor and ill. Dostoyevsky shows dignity kept up under hardship.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky notes that a crowd gathers round a sudden accident partly out of a strange satisfaction, even among those close to the victim. It is a sharp observation of how people watch misfortune.
In Crime and Punishment, a dying man, ashamed of his failings, reaches out to his daughter for forgiveness and falls as he tries. Dostoyevsky shows love and shame arriving together, often too late.
In Crime and Punishment, a grieving little girl who has just lost her father promises to pray for a stranger all her life. Dostoyevsky shows that simple, unguarded kindness can reach someone at their lowest.
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov refuses to let his sister marry for his sake, saying he will not accept the sacrifice. Dostoyevsky shows a love that is proud and unwilling to be repaid with someone else's sacrifice.
In Crime and Punishment, Dostoyevsky observes that a person's true nature shows through whatever their mood, so people soon see what sort of man they are dealing with. Character tends to reveal itself under pressure.
In Crime and Punishment, Razumihin argues that it is better to go wrong in your own way than to go right in someone else's, because a life lived on borrowed ideas is cramped. It is a good reminder to think for yourself.
