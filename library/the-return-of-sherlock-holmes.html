The Return of Sherlock Holmes

Arthur Conan Doyle

430 passages131 things kept

READ · 11 NOVEMBER 2026
PASSAGE 3 OF 430

It can be imagined that my close intimacy with Sherlock Holmes had interested me deeply in crime, and that after his disappearance I never failed to read with care the various problems which came before the public. And I even attempted, more than once, for my own private satisfaction, to employ his methods in their solution, though with indifferent success. There was none, however, which appealed to me like this tragedy of Ronald Adair. As I read the evidence at the inquest, which led up to a verdict of willful murder against some person or persons unknown, I realized more clearly than I had ever done the loss which the community had sustained by the death of Sherlock Holmes. There were points about this strange business which would, I was sure, have specially appealed to him, and the efforts of the police would have been supplemented, or more probably anticipated, by the trained observation and the alert mind of the first criminal agent in Europe. All day, as I drove upon my round, I turned over the case in my mind and found no explanation which appeared to me to be adequate. At the risk of telling a twice-told tale, I will recapitulate the facts as they were known to the public at the conclusion of the inquest.

131 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

In The Empty House, Watson admits his own attempts at Holmes's methods met with only indifferent success, a reminder that careful reasoning is a skill built slowly through practice.
In The Empty House, Watson recalls Holmes's advice that every investigation begins at the line of least resistance, the simplest and most testable path to try first.
In The Empty House, Holmes turns his apparent death into a trap, letting his enemies grow careless while he waits. It is a lesson in patience and in seeing a setback as an opening.
In The Empty House, Holmes tells Watson that work is the best antidote to sorrow, a steadying idea for anyone getting back on their feet after a loss.
In The Empty House, Holmes turns the watchers into the watched, showing that whoever keeps observing a situation calmly and closely often holds the advantage.
In The Empty House, Holmes hands the credit for the arrest to Lestrade, showing that a good leader can let others take the glory and still be respected for the work.
In The Empty House, Holmes cannot act on suspicion alone, so he watches and waits until he can prove the crime. It is a reminder that patience and proof often beat hasty action.
In The Empty House, Holmes admits that even a logical mind can be wrong when it moves beyond the evidence into conjecture, so a sensible theory should be held loosely.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes says that even the faintest trace can reveal a larger pattern to someone who knows what to look for, so small details deserve attention.
In The Norwood Builder, a young solicitor is so flattered and grateful for a sudden windfall that he drops his guard, a useful story about how a generous offer can hide a trap.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes tests Lestrade's neat theory by asking whether a sensible person would really act that way. Stepping into the other person's shoes can expose a story that is too obvious.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes argues that the logical place to start is the earlier, odd event, since understanding it may simplify what followed. Begin with the piece you least understand.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes warns that a mild and respectable appearance proves nothing about a person's guilt or innocence, so judge by evidence rather than manner.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes points out that new evidence is a two-edged thing that may cut in a different direction from the one it first appears to. Check what a fresh fact really supports before acting on it.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes rejects a seemingly final proof because it contradicts what he personally saw earlier. A conclusion, however neat, has to fit the facts you directly observed.
In The Norwood Builder, Holmes says the villain's scheme was ruined by his failure to know when to stop. He kept tightening a plan that was already working, and overreach undid him.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes explains that a deduction looks absurdly simple once each link is laid out. Great reasoning is a chain of small, careful observations.
In The Dancing Men, Hilton Cubitt is sure his wife would do better to trust him, since a frightened silence can cut a couple off from the one person who could help.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes says a sample too short to test gives no real basis for an investigation, so he asks for more evidence before he acts. Wait for enough facts to judge.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes works for two hours alternating between progress and bafflement until the pattern finally gives way. Hard problems yield to sustained, patient effort.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes warns that the absence of a telltale sign proves little, though its presence can be decisive. Be careful about what a missing clue really means.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes finds a bullet hole in the window because he looked for it. Good observation comes from actively searching for what ought to be there, not waiting for it to appear.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes cracks the cipher by taking the most common letter as a working hypothesis, then testing it against the next clue. A provisional guess is useful when it can be checked.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes reasons about who would be writing and what they would likely say, and that context unlocks what letter counts alone could not. Knowing the people involved is part of solving the puzzle.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes holds that whatever one person can invent, another can discover. A clever scheme is only as safe as the patience of the people trying to understand it.
In The Dancing Men, Holmes turns a code used to lure a man into a trap into a message of rescue. Knowledge and skill can serve either side, and the choice of use is what matters.
In The Solitary Cyclist, Holmes narrows a puzzle by exclusion, ruling out each route that cannot be right until only one remains. Eliminating the impossible is often the quickest path to the likely.
In The Solitary Cyclist, Holmes sends Watson to observe the facts for himself and act on his own judgment. Trusting a capable colleague to see for themselves is a fair way to share the work.
In The Solitary Cyclist, Holmes tells Watson that a distant, poorly placed watch learns little, and that real knowledge comes from getting close and asking where local people talk. Go to where the information lives.
In The Solitary Cyclist, Holmes admits plainly that he failed to allow for an earlier train, then turns straight to repairing the damage. Owning a mistake openly and acting at once is a habit worth keeping.
In The Solitary Cyclist, Watson tells Carruthers that his secret watching and his silence, though meant as devotion, look more like selfishness. Caring for someone while hiding the truth from them is a test of honest love.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes tests a theory by asking what a clever deceiver would really have done. When the answer is absurd, he drops the theory but keeps the clue as a starting point.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes keeps asking how a person could know a thing, not just what they believe. Every claim gets traced back to its source.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes narrows a puzzle by closing off possible routes one at a time on a map, using what reliable witnesses saw at each point.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes tells two tyre tracks apart because he has studied so many of them. Deep familiarity with the small details of one's field is what makes a clue readable.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes sets aside a possibility he cannot disprove yet and goes back to the evidence he can check. Leaving a question open is better than forcing an answer.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes stops to separate what is known from what is incidental before pursuing a new lead. Writing down the facts you can trust keeps you from chasing noise.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes treats a missing action as evidence: the master never ran after the boy, so the boy must have had a faster way to escape. What did not happen can tell you as much as what did.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Watson calls the innkeeper a villain on sight, and Holmes reminds him that a sinister manner is not evidence. First impressions are a poor substitute for facts.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes holds that a man who starts a crime is responsible for the other crimes that follow from it. You answer for the consequences of what you set in motion.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, the Duke's secret grows out of one kind act after another, until each concealment demands the next. Shows how a single hidden truth can spread into a family's whole life.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, the Duke keeps yielding to pleas for delay, and each small concession gives the guilty man more time to run. Putting off a hard choice often makes it worse.
In The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes says that to shield a guilty son the Duke has put an innocent son in danger. Protecting the wrong person often costs someone who did nothing.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Watson notes that Holmes turned away rich clients whose problems bored him and gave weeks to humble cases that stirred his imagination. He chose work by what engaged him.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes tests a theory by trying the act himself and finds his own strength cannot do what the theory needs. Test an assumption with your own hands before you believe it.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes holds that anyone who moves through a room leaves some trace, so a careful searcher should always find something. Evidence is usually there if you look for the disturbance.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes admits that a newly found notebook overturns the theory he had built. When a new fact does not fit, the theory has to give way.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes reasons that an intruder who failed with a small blade will come back with a better tool. Asking what someone will try next, based on how they failed, lets you prepare for them.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes says a good investigator always looks for an alternative explanation and prepares for it, even when the first theory seems to be holding. Keep a second line of inquiry open.
In The Adventure of Black Peter, Holmes shows that when a few clues agree, each step narrows the field until only a handful of people fit. He then goes and finds those people directly.
In The Adventure of Charles Augustus Milverton, the blackmailer admits that making an example of one victim keeps all his other debtors paying. His reputation for ruthlessness is what he actually sells.
In The Adventure of Charles Augustus Milverton, Watson refuses to be left behind and tells his friend he will act alone if he must. Loyalty that stands its ground can change the choice of a stubborn friend.
In The Adventure of Charles Augustus Milverton, Holmes grows uneasy when a door is unlocked that should not be. An easier path than expected is a reason to slow down and look again.
In The Adventure of Charles Augustus Milverton, Holmes stops Watson from stepping into a fight that is not theirs to join. He then makes sure the letters that could ruin the innocent are destroyed. Know which duties are yours.
In The Adventure of the Six Napoleons, Holmes refuses to call any small oddity trivial, because some of his most famous cases began with the least promising detail. Take the small strange thing seriously enough to look at it.
In The Adventure of the Six Napoleons, Holmes says an odd fact should be remembered and filed away, since a later discovery may give it meaning. Keep a log of puzzling details, not only the ones that fit today.
In The Adventure of the Six Napoleons, Holmes tells Lestrade to pursue his own line of inquiry while he follows his, then compare notes so each can fill the other's gaps. Two different approaches often cover more ground than one.
In The Adventure of the Six Napoleons, Watson sees that even the coldest reasoner is moved by a friend's honest praise. Behind a sharp mind there is still a person who wants to be appreciated.
In The Adventure of the Six Napoleons, Holmes rebuilds a whole chain of events by working backwards from the known result. Reading the sequence in reverse can show how the pieces connect.
In The Adventure of the Three Students, a key left in a door for a few minutes leads to a serious scandal. Small lapses in routine can cost far more than they seem to at the time.
