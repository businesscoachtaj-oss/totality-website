Jane Eyre

Charlotte Bronte

669 passages140 things kept

READ · 11 NOVEMBER 2026
PASSAGE 2 OF 669

Conventionality is not morality. Self-righteousness is not religion. To attack the first is not to assail the last. To pluck the mask from the face of the Pharisee, is not to lift an impious hand to the Crown of Thorns.

These things and deeds are diametrically opposed: they are as distinct as is vice from virtue. Men too often confound them: they should not be confounded: appearance should not be mistaken for truth; narrow human doctrines, that only tend to elate and magnify a few, should not be substituted for the world-redeeming creed of Christ. There is—I repeat it—a difference; and it is a good, and not a bad action to mark broadly and clearly the line of separation between them.

The world may not like to see these ideas dissevered, for it has been accustomed to blend them; finding it convenient to make external show pass for sterling worth—to let white-washed walls vouch for clean shrines. It may hate him who dares to scrutinise and expose—to rase the gilding, and show base metal under it—to penetrate the sepulchre, and reveal charnel relics: but hate as it will, it is indebted to him.

Ahab did not like Micaiah, because he never prophesied good concerning him, but evil; probably he liked the sycophant son of Chenaanah better; yet might Ahab have escaped a bloody death, had he but stopped his ears to flattery, and opened them to faithful counsel.

140 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

Charlotte Bronte argued that conventionality is not morality and self-righteousness is not religion, and that exposing hypocrisy is a good act, not an assault on faith.
Charlotte Bronte shows how a child with no one to appeal to learns fear, and how adults who look away let a bully grow.
Charlotte Bronte shows a child punished for calling out cruelty, and Jane's first taste of courage is refusing to pretend that what was done to her did not happen.
In Jane Eyre, Bronte shows how favouritism works: the cruel are indulged and the blameless are punished, and a child who is always blamed starts to doubt herself.
Bronte has Jane look back and see clearly what she could not understand as a child, a reminder that hard years often only make sense later.
Bronte shows how one kind visitor with no stake in the household can make a frightened child feel safe, even when the people who should care for her do not.
Bronte notes that children can feel deeply without being able to analyse their feelings, so their hurt often comes out as a clumsy answer that still holds the truth.
Bronte shows how contempt spreads through a household: a child is judged unlovable because nobody bothers to look past her, and the judgment is repeated until it sounds like fact.
In Jane Eyre, Jane begins to refuse the cruel judgments placed on her, a first sign of the self-respect that carries her through the rest of the story.
Charlotte Bronte observes that human beings must love something, and a lonely child will pour her affection into even a shabby doll rather than go without.
Bronte shows how unjust punishment breeds fear so deep that a child hesitates at the door of a room she has every right to enter.
Charlotte Bronte shows Mr. Brocklehurst preaching humility to others while displaying his own pride, a gap between appearance and truth.
Jane speaks the plain truth to Mrs Reed in Jane Eyre and feels a sudden freedom in saying what was done to her, even though it costs her dearly.
Bronte notes that revenge tastes warm at first but leaves a corroding aftertaste, and that a burst of anger is always followed by a chill of regret.
Bronte shows that hardship is easier to bear when it is shared, and that a common grumble among people in the same bad position can be a small comfort.
In Jane Eyre, Miss Temple quietly overrules a harsh routine to feed hungry girls and takes the responsibility herself, a good model of leadership that puts people first.
Bronte shows Helen Burns bearing a public beating with calm dignity while still feeling the hurt privately, a picture of endurance that does not need to pretend it does not hurt.
Helen Burns tells Jane not to judge by appearances, a reminder that the people who look most ordinary or flawed may have the deepest character.
Helen Burns argues that violence does not best overcome hate and that patience and forgiveness heal injury more surely than vengeance, a view Jane finds hard to accept.
Helen Burns argues that life is too short to spend nursing grudges, and that you can forgive the wrongdoer while still condemning the wrong, which keeps revenge from wearing down your heart.
Bronte lets Mr. Brocklehurst lecture girls on plainness while his own wife and daughters arrive in silks and furs, so his hypocrisy is plain to the reader without her needing to say so.
Bronte observes that people who only see small faults are blind to the fuller character of others, a useful check on our own judgments.
Helen Burns helps Jane see that the scorn of a small circle is not the whole world, and that most people may quietly sympathise even when they dare not show it.
Miss Temple gives Jane a fair hearing, asking her to tell the truth plainly, add nothing and exaggerate nothing, and Bronte shows that a calm, honest account is what wins belief.
Once cleared of blame, Jane throws herself into her studies and her progress matches her effort, a clear lesson that a fresh start rewards steady, proportionate work.
Bronte shows that a friend who offers steady, patient affection, never soured by bad moods, is worth more than company that only amuses, and Jane knows it even when she strays.
Bronte shows a failing school turned into a useful one only after its faults were brought into the open and inquired into, a reminder that scrutiny is often what makes an institution better.
When her mentor leaves, Jane finds her calm was partly borrowed, and that steadiness has to rest on reasons she holds herself, not on the person who was steadying her.
With no friends to ask, Jane works out her own plan by stating what she wants, asking how others get it, and acting on the first clear step, which is self-reliance worth copying.
Jane leaves Lowood the proper way: she asks the committee's leave, gets a written testimonial, and only then takes up her new post, showing that doing things in good order protects your reputation.
Facing a new and uncertain start, Jane tells herself she can always try again if the worst comes, a steadying thought that makes a risk feel survivable.
After a hard day, Jane gives thanks and asks for the power to deserve kindness before it is earned, a humble and grateful habit worth keeping.
Jane finds that real equality, not condescension, makes the relationship easier, and notes that a dependent employee can be treated as a friend when respect is genuine.
Jane gained her French through a little practice every day, learning passages by heart and copying her teacher's accent, a reminder that steady small effort builds real skill over time.
Jane refuses to gush about her pupil, saying she is only telling the truth and will not flatter or prop up cant, a plain and honest standard that suits any good account of people.
Charlotte Bronte argues that people need purpose and a field for their abilities or they will find restlessness, and that women feel and need that exercise just as men do.
Jane finds quiet satisfaction in a small act of help that was needed and asked for, a reminder that being useful to someone, however small, lifts the spirit.
In Jane Eyre, Charlotte Bronte shows that a life of safe, unchanging comfort can slowly dull a person, and that a restless wish to be stirred is natural.
In Jane Eyre, Jane meets a moody, rude host with calm self-respect rather than fawning, and finds that composure gives her the upper hand.
In Jane Eyre, Jane says her sketches are pale copies of the vivid pictures in her mind, a gap every maker knows between the idea and the hand that makes it.
In Jane Eyre, Mrs. Fairfax asks for allowance for a difficult person, reminding us that nobody can fully help their nature and that old hurts can unsettle anyone's spirits.
In Jane Eyre, Rochester says hard knocks have left him tough, yet with one tender point still in him, a useful thought about how setbacks can harden us without wholly closing us off.
In Jane Eyre, Jane will not chatter just to show off for a rude man, but when he apologises she accepts it with dignity and offers to answer his questions honestly.
In Jane Eyre, Jane says a person's claim to be superior, whether by age or experience, depends on the use they have made of that time and experience.
In Jane Eyre, Rochester sees that regret alone does not cure a wrong, since only real reform does, though he finds it hard to act on what he knows.
In Jane Eyre, Jane tells Rochester that a stained memory can be replaced over time, since a person who resolves to correct their thoughts and actions builds a new store of recollections to be proud of.
In Jane Eyre, Jane points out that fallible people should not claim the right to excuse an unusual action just by declaring it right, since that power belongs to the perfect alone.
In Jane Eyre, Rochester warns that a smooth stretch of life often ends at a craggy pass where things break into turmoil, and that people come through it either dashed down or carried into calmer water.
In Jane Eyre, Rochester resolves to break the obstacles to his own goodness and to be a better man than he has been, a clear way to frame personal change.
In Jane Eyre, Jane chooses to cling closer to a lonely child rather than a spoilt one, showing that real kindness looks to whoever needs it most.
In Jane Eyre, Jane sees Rochester's faults clearly yet still believes there are good materials in him, a fair and generous way to judge someone whose flaws are plain to see.
In Jane Eyre, Jane keeps her head in a fire, using whatever water is at hand and acting at once rather than waiting for others, which is how calm action in a crisis works.
In Jane Eyre, Grace Poole argues that trusting to luck is no substitute for sensible precautions, since a drawn bolt guards against trouble, a saying worth remembering in business too.
In Jane Eyre, Jane checks her hopes against the plain facts she has, bringing her wandering imagination back to common sense after it has run ahead of the evidence.
In Jane Eyre, Jane steadies a runaway hope by facing her own plain situation, and finds the discipline lets her stay calm when hard news arrives.
In Jane Eyre, the servants note that Grace Poole quietly saves each quarter, a reminder that steady saving over time is what builds independence.
In Jane Eyre, Jane is drawn to quiet, gentle grace over showy rank, a reminder that real dignity is not measured by display.
In Jane Eyre, Jane sees that Blanche Ingram's cleverness is used to belittle a kinder woman, and concludes that talent without good nature is a poor gift.
In Jane Eyre, Jane is drawn to real force of character over polished charm, judging people by their substance rather than by rank or manner.
In Jane Eyre, Jane observes that warmth which comes naturally, with no pretence, draws people in far better than calculated charm, which can only glance off them.
