The Arabian Nights Entertainments

Andrew Lang (editor)

PASSAGE 2 OF 385

The stories in the Fairy Books have generally been such as old women in country places tell to their grandchildren. Nobody knows how old they are, or who told them first. The children of Ham, Shem and Japhet may have listened to them in the Ark, on wet days. Hector's little boy may have heard them in Troy Town, for it is certain that Homer knew them, and that some of them were written down in Egypt about the time of Moses.

People in different countries tell them differently, but they are always the same stories, really, whether among little Zulus, at the Cape, or little Eskimo, near the North Pole. The changes are only in matters of manners and customs; such as wearing clothes or not, meeting lions who talk in the warm countries, or talking bears in the cold countries. There are plenty of kings and queens in the fairy tales, just because long ago there were plenty of kings in the country. A gentleman who would be a squire now was a kind of king in Scotland in very old times, and the same in other places. These old stories, never forgotten, were taken down in writing in different ages, but mostly in this century, in all sorts of languages. These ancient stories are the contents of the Fairy books.

106 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

Andrew Lang notes that old stories outlive their tellers, passed from grandmothers to children in every country and changed only in small customs.
In The Arabian Nights, the tales are framed as a wife telling stories to a cruel Sultan, so the storyteller's craft becomes a way of staying alive.
In The Arabian Nights, the Sultan's grief at one betrayal hardens into a rule that punishes innocent people, and a whole town pays for it.
In The Arabian Nights, Scheherazade chooses a deadly risk on purpose, with a plan, to end a cruelty that everyone else has simply accepted.
In The Arabian Nights, Scheherazade brings her sister into her plan before she acts, turning a private fear into a shared task with someone she trusts.
In The Arabian Nights, a merchant asks for a year to settle his affairs and gives his word to return, and the genius trusts that word.
Before facing his fate, the merchant in The Arabian Nights pays his debts and provides for his family, so he leaves his affairs in order.
In The Arabian Nights, an old man's pity for an animal's tears leads him to refuse a cruel order, and his wife's scorn cannot change his mind.
In The Arabian Nights, a merchant who has recovered his fortune shares half of it with his ruined brother and tells him to forget his losses.
In The Arabian Nights, a merchant shares his savings with brothers who squandered their own, and does not reproach them, a generosity that outlasts their folly.
In The Arabian Nights, the merchant is spared because three storytellers take the trouble to speak up for him, which shows how much a kind word can do.
In The Arabian Nights, the fisherman outwits a boastful genius by asking him to prove his claim, showing that a calm question can turn a threat around.
In The Arabian Nights, a husband kills his parrot for lying, then finds it told the truth, a reminder not to act in haste on a doubtful word.
In The Arabian Nights, a jealous vizir dresses up his envy as concern for the king, and the weak king listens. Good advisers are worth testing.
In The Arabian Nights, a cruel king who has an innocent man killed is undone by his own greed for a trick, and the dying man's last words name the lesson: cruelty comes back on the cruel.
In The Arabian Nights, the Sultan goes alone to solve a mystery himself rather than sending others, and his court waits in the tent, a picture of a leader who checks the facts first-hand.
In The Arabian Nights, the Sultan is moved by a stranger's suffering and sets out to put it right, so his power is shown to be used well.
In The Arabian Nights, a house warns over its door that whoever meddles in other people's affairs will hear truths they do not like, a rule any host or team could still use.
In The Arabian Nights, the porter tells a rude guest that not everyone has to live the same way, a plain reminder that other people's choices are not ours to judge.
In The Arabian Nights, the porter begs not to be punished for other people's faults, a plea for fairness that even the angry hostess finds hard to refuse.
In The Arabian Nights, a prince breaks a secret oath because keeping it would cost his uncle his son, showing that some promises must give way to a greater duty.
In The Arabian Nights, a prince who has lost everything takes up honest work cutting wood, so he can provide for himself until better times come.
In The Arabian Nights, a prince refuses to kill an innocent stranger even with his own life at stake, saying he would not deserve to live if he were that kind of coward.
In The Arabian Nights, an envious man plots to ruin the neighbour he resents, and his plan fails, showing how envy wastes the person who carries it.
In The Arabian Nights, a dervish who was once nearly killed by an envious neighbour answers him with honour and reward rather than revenge, a picture of forgiveness that goes beyond mere pardon.
In The Arabian Nights, the Sultan judges writing by its quality and sets aside the reputations of the famous writers, a fair way to weigh any work.
In The Arabian Nights, the clever princess says there are many out-of-the-way things it is good to know, but one should never boast of them. Skill is quieter when it is not shown off.
In The Arabian Nights, the freed prince says he would rather have stayed a monkey than see the princess who saved him die, showing loyalty that counts for more than his own comfort.
In The Arabian Nights, Agib forgets the warning he was given the moment relief sweeps over him, and the warning is what undoes him. Relief is when we are most likely to slip.
In The Arabian Nights, Agib gives up his plan to harm the frightened youth and offers him friendship instead, turning a feared enemy into a companion.
In The Arabian Nights, ten young men blame their own idleness for their ruin, showing how a person can look honestly at the cost of a wasted life.
In The Arabian Nights, Agib talks himself into opening a forbidden door by telling himself that looking is not really entering. The story shows how curiosity can argue down a clear conscience.
In The Arabian Nights, the rich Sindbad tells the envious porter that his wealth came from years of toil and danger, which no one sees from the street where the envy starts.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad admits he squandered his inheritance on pleasure, and that money managed badly flies away, so he turned to honest work at sea.
In The Arabian Nights, the captain who kept a lost man's goods refuses any reward for his honesty, a plain picture of integrity that asks nothing back.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad grows restless with an idle life, sails again, and is stranded, regretting that he did not stay content. Restlessness can cost us the safety we had.
In Sindbad's second voyage, the narrator shares his diamonds with the men who had been cheated of their trade, and they warm to him. Generosity can turn rivals into friends.
In Sindbad's second voyage, a merchant takes only one stone from the great heap, saying that one is enough to end his toil. The story is a lesson in knowing when you have enough.
In Sindbad's third voyage, the stranded sailors turn despair into action: Sindbad lays out a plan to escape and a fallback with rafts if it fails. A backup plan steadies people when all seems lost.
In Sindbad's third voyage, a stranded man who wants to live builds a shelter piece by piece through the day, and it holds through the night. Steady effort is what gets people through.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad's companions eat the food of their captors until they lose their wits, while Sindbad tastes only a little and stays clear-headed. Restraint keeps a person safe.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad earns respect in a strange land by noticing a real need and making something useful for it, here a saddle for the king's riders.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad speaks up against a cruel custom that is not his own, and the king's easy acceptance of it shows how habit can hide cruelty from those who live by it.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad is saved in a dark pit by a small animal that shows him a way out he could never have found alone, so help can come from unexpected places.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad admits that being alone in a strange land makes him wish he had stayed home, and that morning restores his courage. Fear often feels larger in the dark.
In the Sindbad story, a clinging old tyrant is beaten not by force but by a simple trick that turns his own greed against him. Cleverness can outlast strength.
In the Sindbad story, the captain shares out the last of the food equally before anything else. When supplies run short, a fair split keeps a group together.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad, with little left and no way forward, notices a river running underground and reasons that trying costs him nothing he does not already lose. Hope often starts with a careful look at what is left.
In The Arabian Nights, the Caliph judges the king of Serendib to be wise from his letter alone, reasoning that a good ruler and a worthy people reflect one another.
In The Arabian Nights, the master who has profited from Sindbad's work sets him free and rewards him. Fair dealing is often paid back, even by people who once held power over you.
In The Arabian Nights, Sindbad bears a long, dull caravan journey with patience, reflecting on the dangers he has already escaped. Gratitude makes a tedious stretch easier to carry.
In The Little Hunchback, one accident leads to a chain of lies and cover-ups, each worse than the last. A single concealment can drag innocent people into trouble.
In The Little Hunchback, the purveyor steps forward in public to confess, even though it costs him, because he cannot bear for an innocent man to die for his fault. Owning up to a wrong can stop an injustice.
In the story of Alnaschar, a glassware seller plans his whole fortune in his head before he has sold a single bottle. Dreaming ahead is not the same as doing the work, and the plan has no foundations.
In the Alnaschar story, pride makes a man careless, and only the wreck of his basket brings him back to his senses. Vanity can cost more than the thing it was meant to gain.
In the Alnaschar story, a man is so eager to believe in an easy fortune that he follows a stranger into a trap. A promise that seems too good to be true usually deserves a second look.
In the Barmecide's feast, a hungry beggar plays along with an imaginary banquet, and his host rewards his good humour with a real welcome. Going along gracefully with someone's game can earn lasting goodwill.
In the Barber's tale, a man who looks closely at the evidence before accepting a verdict reveals the truth. Careful examination can overturn a hasty judgment.
In the Camaralzaman tale, a wise vizir advises that a young heir learn to rule by sharing in counsel first, rather than being handed power all at once. Responsibility grows when it is given step by step.
In the Camaralzaman tale, the vizir reminds the king that much is gained by patience, and that harsh force is often regretted. Waiting can get a better result than pushing harder.
