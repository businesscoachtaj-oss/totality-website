A Christmas Carol

Charles Dickens

PASSAGE 3 OF 102

Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grind-stone, Scrooge! a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster. The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek, stiffened his gait; made his eyes red, his thin lips blue; and spoke out shrewdly in his grating voice. A frosty rime was on his head, and on his eyebrows, and his wiry chin. He carried his own low temperature always about with him; he iced his office in the dog-days; and didn't thaw it one degree at Christmas.

External heat and cold had little influence on
Scrooge. No warmth could warm, no wintry weather
chill him. No wind that blew was bitterer than he,
no falling snow was more intent upon its purpose, no
pelting rain less open to entreaty. Foul weather didn't
know where to have him. The heaviest rain, and
snow, and hail, and sleet, could boast of the advantage
over him in only one respect. They often "came down"
handsomely, and Scrooge never did.

Nobody ever stopped him in the street to say, with
gladsome looks, "My dear Scrooge, how are you?
When will you come to see me?" No beggars implored
him to bestow a trifle, no children asked him
what it was o'clock, no man or woman ever once in all
his life inquired the way to such and such a place, of
Scrooge. Even the blind men's dogs appeared to
know him; and when they saw him coming on, would
tug their owners into doorways and up courts; and
then would wag their tails as though they said, "No
eye at all is better than an evil eye, dark master!"

61 things from this book went into her brain. Here are the first 6.

In A Christmas Carol, Dickens shows how a man who shuts out every warm connection grows so cold that even strangers and dogs keep their distance from him.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew answers his uncle's sneers with good humour rather than anger, and the warmth holds its ground.
In A Christmas Carol, the nephew argues that kindness is worth keeping even when it brings no money, because it lets people open their shut-up hearts to others.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew keeps offering friendship without anger, showing that steady kindness can outlast a long refusal.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge hands the poor off to prisons and workhouses and says it is not his business, a way of passing on responsibility that Dickens exposes as cold.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens shows Scrooge docking a day's pay for a holiday and calling it fair, showing how a hard employer can persuade himself his stinginess is principle.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens describes Scrooge's gloomy rooms as a place that has forgotten the way out, showing how a life closed to others becomes its own dark prison.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge spends the day refusing to think of his dead partner, yet the thought returns in the knocker, showing that avoided worries do not stay buried.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge keeps calling the ghost humbug even as it passes through his door, showing how a mind will cling to denial long after the senses have given way.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens makes Marley's chain out of the cash-boxes, ledgers and purses he hoarded in life, showing that the things we grasp for ourselves can become the chains we drag.
In A Christmas Carol, Marley's ghost says the spirit within a person must go out among others in life, or it is doomed to wander after death, seeing what it could have shared.
In A Christmas Carol, Marley's ghost says his real business was mankind and the common welfare, and his trade was only a drop of water in that ocean. Dickens offers this as a test for any owner.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens shows phantoms who want to help people but have lost the power to act, suggesting that good intentions left unpractised become a hollow regret.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens notes that the more Scrooge tries not to think about the ghost, the more he thinks, showing how pushing away an unsettling question only keeps it turning.
In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Past tells Scrooge he needs reclamation and must take heed. Dickens treats honest looking at one's own past as the first step towards change.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge recognises every gate and tree on the road of his boyhood, showing that the people we once were are still close, even after we have forgotten the way back to them.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge is moved to tears by a neglected child left alone at school, showing how a hard heart can still be opened by seeing a lonely child.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge weeps at the sight of his lonely boyhood self, showing that the softest pity often starts with seeing our own forgotten self clearly.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge regrets that he did not give the carol singer something. The Ghost lets him see it, showing that regret for a small unkindness can be the start of a better habit.
In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost calls Fan, though delicate, a woman with a large heart, showing Dickens' view that a person is measured by love rather than by strength.
In A Christmas Carol, old Fezziwig ends work early on Christmas Eve so his staff can celebrate. Dickens uses him to show that a good employer's generosity earns loyalty.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge realises a boss's power lies in small words and looks, and that the happiness those bring can be as great as if they had cost a fortune.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's first love tells him another idol, a golden one, has displaced her. Dickens shows how the pursuit of gain can quietly push out the people we once cared for.
In A Christmas Carol, the girl tells Scrooge he has let his nobler hopes fall away one by one until gain took over. It is a warning about how small compromises add up over a life.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's former love releases him with a full heart, rather than hold him to a promise he no longer keeps. Dickens shows that letting go can be a kindness.
In A Christmas Carol, Belle's household greets her husband with noisy, unguarded delight. Dickens reminds us that a warm home is built on affection rather than wealth.
In A Christmas Carol, Belle's husband notes that Scrooge sits quite alone in the world. Scrooge cannot bear to watch, showing that loneliness is easier to see in others than to admit in ourselves.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens observes that it is always the person outside the predicament who knows what ought to have been done in it, a sharp reminder to be humble when judging others' hard choices.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge tells the Ghost of Christmas Present that the lesson of the night before is already working on him, showing how one honest reckoning can change a person's conduct.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens says a playful snowball is a better-natured missile than many a wordy joke, a small reminder that good humour often travels through simple, friendly acts.
In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Present says a kindly given dinner matters most to a poor person because it is needed most. Dickens makes generosity a matter of need, not status.
The Ghost of Christmas Present says people who do cruelty in the name of a cause should have their deeds charged to themselves, not to the cause. It is a useful principle when judging any movement.
In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost blesses a poor clerk's small house, a reminder that a home's worth is not measured by its size.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens notes that the Cratchits' modest goose is enough for everyone. Contentment, not abundance, is what fills a family table.
The Ghost of Christmas Present asks Scrooge who he is to decide which men shall live and which shall die. It is a lasting warning against judging whose lives are worth more.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens says the Cratchits were happy and grateful with one another despite their poverty. Contentment with what one has matters more than appearance.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens shows people hurrying to gatherings where every house has its fires built up for company. Hospitality is a choice each household makes.
In A Christmas Carol, an old miner's spirit rises when his family joins his song and sinks when they stop. It is a plain picture of how shared effort lifts us.
Dickens describes sailors far from land giving one another a kinder word on Christmas, showing that goodwill can reach even the loneliest places.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens observes that laughter and good humour are even more contagious than sorrow, a useful thought for anyone who leads a team.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew pities his uncle rather than resenting him, saying the one who suffers most from a grudge is the person who holds it. It is a calm, forgiving stance.
In A Christmas Carol, the nephew resolves to offer his uncle goodwill every year, whether he likes it or not. Steady, patient kindness is the best answer to someone who keeps refusing it.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge realises that he might have cultivated life's kindnesses with his own hands. Kindness is a practice we build, not a mood we wait for.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge forgets for a while that no one can hear him and joins the games with real joy. A closed heart can open once it is absorbed in other people.
In A Christmas Carol, the Spirit finds cheerfulness at sick beds and patience among struggling men. Dickens suggests that the spirit of a home matters more than its circumstances.
In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost names Ignorance and Want as children that cling to humanity, and warns that neglecting them writes a doom that can still be erased.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge faces the silent Ghost of Yet To Come and says he hopes to become another man than he was. Willingness to face hard truths is where change begins.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens has businessmen discuss a dead man with cold indifference, joking about who will attend the funeral. A life spent chasing gain can leave no one to mourn it.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge resolves to watch closely and learn from what he sees of his own future conduct. It is a good instinct for anyone who wants to learn from the consequences of their choices.
In A Christmas Carol, a thief says the dead miser would have had someone at his bedside had he been kind in life. The company we keep at the end is shaped by how we treated people earlier.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge sees in a dead miser's lonely end the shape of his own future. Those who push everyone away in life may be robbed by strangers in death.
In A Christmas Carol, Dickens says a life is judged by the kind deeds that outlast it, not by the wealth it leaves behind. Scrooge is shown a hand that was open, generous and true.
In A Christmas Carol, a family is relieved by a creditor's death. Dickens shows how harsh dealing can leave a man unmourned and the people he pressed quietly glad to be free.
In A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit family bear Tiny Tim's absence with quiet love. Dickens shows that hard times are borne best when people lean on one another and keep speaking with steady cheer.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew helps the Cratchits mostly through the warmth of his manner, not through any favour it might bring. Sincere care is felt more than any help.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge argues that if a course of life is departed from, its ends will change. Dickens makes the future open to a changed life.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge resolves to hold the lessons of past, present and future together. Learn from what has been, act in what is, and keep what may come in view.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge sends a large turkey to the Cratchits anonymously. Dickens shows that the joy of giving comes from a changed heart, not from being thanked.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge makes amends to the gentleman he once turned away by apologising and giving generously. The best repair is to act on the wrong, not only to regret it.
In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge raises his clerk's pay and offers to help the struggling family. It shows how an employer can turn a change of heart into concrete fairness.
