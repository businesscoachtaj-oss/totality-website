What Tali kept

95 things from this book went into her brain so far. Here are the first 6.

Charles Darwin's opening quotes hold that events come about through general laws rather than separate interventions of divine power, and that a person should keep studying both the book of God's word and the book of God's works. Keep learning widely and never stop.
Darwin observed that every species can increase far faster than its food and space allow, so competition is universal. The fiercest struggle is usually between individuals and varieties of the same species, not between different kinds of creatures.
Darwin noted that traits that are used grow stronger and those left unused tend to fade, and that changes in one part of a body often bring changes in another. Competition among small differences is what shapes which forms survive.
Darwin spent years gathering and reflecting on facts before he trusted his conclusions, and he said so openly to show he had not been hasty. Careful, patient evidence-gathering comes before a firm decision.
Darwin held back his work for years, but he chose to publish a shorter version once another researcher had reached much the same ideas. Sharing your work in time matters more than waiting for it to be perfect.
Darwin held that a conclusion about descent is unsatisfying until you can show how the modifications arose. Explaining fine adaptation by a vague outside cause is not enough.
Darwin held that the best way to understand how living things change is to study domesticated animals and cultivated plants, which show variation openly. He thought this was neglected but valuable.
Darwin held that more individuals are born than can survive, so any slight variation that helps an individual has a better chance and is passed on. Small advantages accumulate over generations.
Darwin held that our knowledge of how living things depend on one another is so limited that much about the origin of species will stay unexplained for a long time. He still saw that these relations shape the future of every living thing.
Darwin argued that species are not fixed but descend from earlier, often extinct, species, much as varieties descend from their parent species. He saw natural selection as the main, though not the only, means of change.
Darwin observed that cultivated plants and animals vary more among themselves than wild species do, likely because they are raised under less uniform conditions. A change in conditions has to last several generations before it produces noticeable variation.
Darwin suspected that most variation starts in the reproductive elements before conception, since confinement and cultivation disturb breeding far more than other bodily functions. Changes in conditions can quietly stop an organism from reproducing.
Darwin noted that 'sporting' buds, which suddenly differ from the rest of a plant, are rare in nature but common under cultivation. He took this as support for the idea that variation comes from the parent's treatment before reproduction.
Darwin observed that seedlings and young from the same parents can differ noticeably even under identical conditions. So the direct effect of food, light and climate is usually small compared with the laws of reproduction and inheritance.
Darwin held that use and disuse shape a body over generations, so a domestic duck with less flying and more walking ends up with lighter wings and heavier legs. Habit can therefore leave a lasting mark that is passed on.
Charles Darwin noted that changing one part of an animal often changes other, distinct parts too, so a breeder who selects for one trait will likely alter others without meaning to.
Charles Darwin argued that inheritance should be treated as the normal rule and non-inheritance as the exception, since rare traits that reappear in a family are unlikely to be coincidence.
Charles Darwin observed that a trait tends to show up in offspring at the same stage of life it first appeared in the parent, a rule he thought central to understanding embryology.
Darwin argued that the claim that domestic varieties always revert to wild ancestors is unproven. Reversion is possible, but the outcome depends on changed conditions, and natural selection decides which new traits survive.
Darwin held that domestic races of one species differ from each other much as closely related species do in nature, only usually to a lesser degree. Experts often disagree whether a given form is a variety or a distinct species.
Darwin noted that it is hard to measure how different domestic races are when we do not know whether they came from one parent species or several. Settling that question would bear on whether closely allied wild species are truly fixed.
Darwin doubted the idea that people chose animals and plants for domestication because they were unusually variable or hardy. Early humans could not have known such traits in advance, and any species given time under breeding would vary much as domesticated ones have.
Darwin admitted that the origin of most ancient domestic animals and plants cannot be settled, since old records show diversity that may have arisen long before those records. Ancient evidence can only show when a breed was already known, not how it began.
Darwin warned that it is absurd to assume a separate wild ancestor for every slight, true-breeding variety. Inherited variation within a species can explain much of what looks like many separate origins.
Darwin held that crossing two very different breeds rarely gives a stable new one, since the mongrels of later generations differ widely. Producing a lasting new form takes careful, long-continued selection of the best individuals.
Charles Darwin held that choosing one special group to study closely, such as domestic pigeons, is the best way to learn general truths. He kept every breed he could obtain and talked with expert fanciers.
Charles Darwin noticed that many separate features of a body, from bones to feathers to eggs, can each vary independently. Careful study means looking at all of these traits, not just the most obvious one.
Charles Darwin reasoned by testing each rival explanation against the evidence. He asked whether the supposed original stocks could still exist unnoticed, or could have vanished, and found the second story hard to believe.
Darwin held that when many unlikely coincidences must all be true for a claim to work, the claim becomes improbable in the highest degree. He weighed how many strange chances a theory needed, not just whether it could be true.
Darwin held that when a trait keeps reappearing after crossbreeding, the simplest explanation is that the birds share a common ancestor, since the trait is hidden in their family history. Competing explanations often need several improbable assumptions to hold at once.
Darwin held that when several separate pieces of evidence each point the same way, taken together they can justify a conclusion that no single piece could. He also warned against stretching a hypothesis, such as easy fertility between distinct kinds, without any direct test.
Darwin held that a claim gains strength when it is backed by many independent lines of evidence, such as a smooth series of forms, traits that vary widely within a group, and long human records. He treated careful watching and patient breeding over time as a real source of knowledge.
Darwin noticed that breeders are firmly sure their breeds came from separate species, yet they accept slight differences as they win prizes by them. He urged caution before dismissing an idea just because its steps are too small to see.
Darwin held that the great variety of useful domestic breeds comes from accumulative selection: nature supplies small variations, and people add them up in the directions they find useful, one generation at a time.
Darwin pointed out that skilled breeders treat a living form as something they can shape steadily over the years. Patient, repeated selection, judged again and again, can change a flock far more than most people expect.
Darwin observed that the real gains came from small differences, too fine for an untrained eye, piled up over many generations. Mastery needs a sharp eye, years of study and sustained perseverance, and most people will not reach it.
Darwin noted that different varieties of one plant species often differ sharply in one valued part, such as leaves, flowers or fruit, while looking nearly alike in others. Choosing one trait over generations can reshape that part alone.
Darwin argued that the principle of selection was not a modern discovery, since ancient writers and early breeders already valued it. Careful attention to what gets passed on is an old and common practice.
Darwin held that unconscious selection works quietly: each person who keeps and breeds from their best individuals slowly changes a line over time, even without meaning to. Small steady choices add up to large change.
Charles Darwin observed that small, steady choices over generations can make animals and plants very different from their origins, and that breeders following the same method can end up with two quite different strains.
Charles Darwin held that people often keep the most useful animal alive in hard times, so the best individuals leave more offspring and a quiet selection happens even without anyone intending it.
Charles Darwin argued that gardeners improved fruit and flowers by always growing and saving seed from the best plant they had, so that progress came slowly and almost unconsciously.
Charles Darwin noted that improvement built up over centuries is why the wild ancestors of many garden plants are now hard to recognise at all.
Charles Darwin held that breeders can only select from variations nature first provides, and that the breeder rarely sets out to build a final form, since the first small deviation is what catches the eye.
Charles Darwin observed that people notice very small differences and value any novelty they own, so a slight change can matter far more to a keen eye than it would to an outsider.
Charles Darwin noted that improvements usually build up slowly and unrecorded, like a dialect, so the true history of a gradual change is often lost even when its results are plain to see.
Charles Darwin argued that improvement comes from keeping many individuals and paying close attention to small differences, since useful variations appear only now and then and are easily neglected.
Darwin noted that breeders succeed more easily when they can keep different lines apart and mate animals reliably, since pigeons pair for life and can be bred quickly in numbers. Breeds only form where someone actually chooses which animals to keep and breed.
Darwin held that the accumulated effect of selection, whether done deliberately and quickly or unconsciously and slowly, is the strongest force shaping change. Many other causes add to this, but they are harder to predict and matter less.
Darwin admitted that no one has yet given a definition of a species that satisfies every naturalist, yet people still use the word with a rough shared understanding. Good working terms can stay useful even when their exact edges are disputed.
Darwin noted that individual differences within one species are common, and that even important-looking parts vary. He warned that people who assume important features never vary are arguing in a circle.
Darwin observed that experts often dislike finding variation in what they consider key traits, so they seldom examine internal organs across many specimens. Careful, patient checking often shows more variety than expected.
Darwin observed that when two forms are linked by intermediate examples, scientists usually class one as a variety of the other. He warned that assumptions about missing links open a wide door to doubt and guesswork.
Darwin held that when judging whether a form is a species or a variety, the opinion of experienced, sound-judging experts is the best guide. Where they disagree, a majority view is often the practical answer.
Darwin noticed that experts often disagree on whether a form is a species or a mere variety, so the line between them is vague and arbitrary. Ask two careful judges and you may get two different answers.
Darwin held that arguing over whether a doubtful form is a species or a variety is pointless until the terms are clearly defined. Settle what you mean by the words before you argue about the answer.
Darwin weighed a case from many angles, such as appearance, habitat, range and crossing, and found the evidence pointing in different directions. Judge a hard question by gathering several kinds of evidence, not by one striking feature.
Darwin observed that the things people care most about get studied most, and so they collect the most disputed labels. Close, patient investigation usually brings careful people to agreement in the end.
Darwin observed that a beginner tends to make too many species, because they are impressed by small differences and lack wider knowledge. Only by studying widely does one learn to tell varieties from species, and even then at the cost of admitting more variation.
Darwin held that there is no clear dividing line between species, sub-species, varieties and individual differences, which blend into one another in an unbroken series. Each slight difference can be seen as a first step towards a more marked one.
