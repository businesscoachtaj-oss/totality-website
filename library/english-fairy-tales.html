English Fairy Tales

Joseph Jacobs (editor)

PASSAGE 3 OF 212

The first adjective of our title also needs a similar extension of its meaning. I have acted on Molière's principle, and have taken what was good wherever I could find it. Thus, a couple of these stories have been found among descendants of English immigrants in America; a couple of others I tell as I heard them myself in my youth in Australia. One of the best was taken down from the mouth of an English Gipsy. I have also included some stories that have only been found in Lowland Scotch. I have felt justified in doing this, as of the twenty-one folk-tales contained in Chambers' “Popular Rhymes of Scotland,” no less than sixteen are also to be found in an English form. With the Folk-tale as with the Ballad, Lowland Scotch may be regarded as simply a dialect of English, and it is a mere chance whether a tale is extant in one or other, or both.

I have also rescued and re-told a few Fairy Tales that only exist now-a-days in the form of ballads. There are certain indications that the “common form” of the English Fairy Tale was the cante-fable, a mixture of narrative and verse of which the most illustrious example in literature is “Aucassin et Nicolette.” In one case I have endeavoured to retain this form, as the tale in which it occurs, “Childe Rowland,” is mentioned by Shakespeare in King Lear, and is probably, as I have shown, the source of Milton's Comus. Late as they have been collected, some dozen of the tales can be traced back to the sixteenth century, two of them being quoted by Shakespeare himself.

Joseph Jacobs, collecting English fairy tales, took what was good wherever he could find it, from Scotland, America and an English Gipsy alike.
In Tom Tit Tot, a clever girl escapes a cruel bargain by quietly listening to the impet's secret, then turning what she overheard into her own victory.
In The Three Sillies, a family weeps over a harm that has not happened yet, and the traveller's lesson is that fretting over imagined troubles is its own kind of foolishness.
In How Jack Went to Seek His Fortune, a boy's ordinary animal companions scare off robbers by all raising their voices together, showing that a small team working as one can beat a bigger threat.
In Mr. Vinegar, a man keeps trading what he has for the next thing that he is sure will make him happiest, until a parrot shows him he has gained nothing. Chasing the next shiny thing can cost you what you already had.
In Nix Nought Nothing, a giant's daughter helps the young man through impossible tasks, even giving up her own fingers and toes to make steps for him, showing how loyal courage can cost a great deal.
In Binnorie, a jealous sister's crime comes to light when a harp made from her victim's bones sings out the truth, showing how jealousy can hide a wrong only for a while.
In Cap o' Rushes, a father who rewards flattery drives out the daughter who speaks plainly, yet her honest worth shows through in hard, humble work.
In Jack and the Beanstalk, Jack takes a gamble on strange beans that his mother calls foolish, and that bold step opens a road to the sky that no sensible bargain could have reached.
In The Three Little Pigs, the pig who took the slow, solid route with bricks is the one who stays safe, and his early preparation lets his wits beat the wolf's tricks.
In The Master and His Pupil, a foolish lad summons a power he cannot control because he never learned how to send it away. Know how to stop a thing before you start it.
In Jack and His Golden Snuff-box, Jack is handed a helper with the rule to use it only when it truly matters, and when the hard moment comes it turns a deadline he could not meet into a win.
In Jack and His Golden Snuff-box, a little mouse that Jack spared says it may do him good someday without him knowing, and it does. Small kindnesses have a way of coming back.
In Jack and His Golden Snuff-box, the travelling companions drop the treasure when they quarrel over who deserves the credit, a neat lesson that squabbling over the win can lose the win itself.
In The Three Bears, the intruder tries each porridge, chair and bed until one is just right. Finding what fits often means testing a few options patiently, until the right one shows itself.
In Jack the Giant-Killer, a captive keeps his nerve, studies the room and finds the rope that turns the tables on his captors. Staying calm and paying attention is often the way out.
In Jack the Giant-Killer, a prince gives away everything he has to the people his father's town owes, and the story shows generosity as a quiet kind of strength that others will follow.
In Jack the Giant-Killer, Jack rides ahead to clear the way for his master, handling the danger first so the prince can arrive safely. Good support often means going first to deal with the hard part.
In Henny-Penny, a flock of barnyard birds follows a fox who offers a shortcut, and none of them asks whether he really knows the way. Check who you are trusting before you follow them somewhere.
In Childe Rowland, the two elder brothers set off without learning the rules and are lost, while the youngest is taught the rules, repeats them until he knows them by heart, and comes back. Prepare properly before you go.
In Childe Rowland, the hero is tempted by a bowl of food when he is hungry, but he remembers his purpose and refuses it, a neat picture of holding to your commitments when comfort tempts you away.
In Molly Whuppie, the youngest of three sisters outwits a giant by swapping the rope and gold charms while he sleeps, showing that quick thinking can save the people you love in a bad spot.
In Molly Whuppie, the clever girl goes back into the giant's house twice more for the sword and the purse, each time bold enough to try again after a near miss.
In Molly Whuppie, a small and clever girl beats a giant by her wits, turning his own threats back on him rather than meeting force with force.
In The Red Ettin, the younger brother patches his leaking can with clay and then chooses his mother's blessing over a bigger cake with her curse. Good sense and good character go together.
In The Red Ettin, the younger brother shares his johnny-cake with an old woman, and her gift of a magic wand later saves him. Kindness comes back round.
In The Red Ettin, the young man survives because he took the good fairy's advice and listened to it. Preparation and humility beat bravado.
In The Golden Arm, a man loves his dead wife's gold arm more than her, and his greed is undone when her ghost asks him the one question he cannot dodge.
In Tom Thumb, the boy's cunning turns to thieving, and each clever trick lands him in worse trouble. Being clever is not the same as being good.
In Tom Thumb, a court favourite's rise is fragile, and one spiteful word from the queen brings the little knight to ruin. Favour can vanish overnight.
In Mr. Fox, Lady Mary exposes a murderer by telling her dream calmly to his face, with the proof hidden in her dress. Courage can be quiet and clever.
In Lazy Jack, the boy does exactly what he is told and never what is meant, always promising to do better another time. Understanding what is actually wanted is a skill.
In The Johnny-Cake, the boastful cake outruns everyone who chases it, then trusts a fox's flattery and is eaten. Pride tends to go before a fall.
In Earl Mar's Daughter, the girl does not panic at a stranger in her locked room. She asks him plainly who he is and how he got in, and her courage opens the story.
In Earl Mar's Daughter, the girl keeps faith with her secret husband through seven years and holds to her own choice when her father demands another.
In Mr. Miacca, Tommy Grimes is saved by his quick wit when the ogre has him. Cleverness can get you out of a bind, but it is better not to need it.
In Dick Whittington, a poor orphan finds the courage to ask the waggoner for a ride towards London. That small brave step starts his whole story.
In Dick Whittington, Alice tells the cruel cook she will be turned away unless she treats the boy kindly. A word from someone with standing can stop cruelty.
In Dick Whittington, the boy gives up his only possession, a cat, to the captain's venture with tears in his eyes. Giving what you have, however little, is how a chance begins.
In Dick Whittington, the boy turns back from giving up when he hears the bells urge him to return. Staying with the work, instead of running from it, is what changes his luck.
In Dick Whittington, the merchant Mr. Fitzwarren insists the treasure belongs wholly to Dick and refuses to take a penny of it. Honesty about what is rightly someone else's marks a good man.
In Dick Whittington, the boy shares his good fortune with the captain, the servants and even the cruel old cook. Generosity after success is the mark of a kind heart.
In The Strange Visitor, a woman who keeps wishing aloud for company gets a visitor far from what she pictured. Be careful what you wish for.
In The Laidly Worm of Spindleston Heugh, Childe Wynd does not turn away from the monster. Three kisses from a loving brother break the spell and bring his sister back.
In The Fish and the Ring, a band of robbers reads a murderous letter, thinks it a shame, and rewrites it so the girl is saved. A small act of conscience can turn a cruel plan around.
In The Fish and the Ring, a cruel baron tries to escape a fate he feared, and his own plots bring it about. Forcing the future through cruelty tends to backfire.
In The Magpie's Nest, the birds leave the teacher before the lesson is finished, so each one learns only a piece. Staying for the whole lesson is how you really learn.
In Kate Crackernuts, Kate is brave enough to sit up alone with a mysterious sick prince, and her quiet watchfulness reveals what is really happening.
In The Ass, the Table, and the Stick, Jack's magic rewards are swapped for worthless copies while he is not looking. Check what you have been given before you rely on it.
In The Ass, the Table, and the Stick, Jack's new power turns him cruel, and he strikes the poor sweetheart who had nothing to give him. Power without kindness goes sour.
In Fairy Ointment, Dame Goody's curiosity costs her the sight of one eye when she lets the pixy see she can see him. Some things are best left unremarked.
In The Well of the World's End, the girl keeps her promise to the frog who helped her, even though it costs her. Keeping your word to those who help you is a good habit.
In The Three Heads of the Well, a princess tends three golden heads with patient care, and they reward her with beauty, a sweet voice and a royal marriage.
In The Three Heads of the Well, the stepsister who refuses to share her bread and wine with the old man earns his curse. Being mean with what you have costs you.
In The Three Heads of the Well, a poor cobbler helps a cursed stranger purely out of charity, and that plain kindness is what changes her life.
