PASSAGE 3 OF 229

“Really, Watson, you excel yourself,” said Holmes, pushing back his chair and lighting a cigarette. “I am bound to say that in all the accounts which you have been so good as to give of my own small achievements you have habitually underrated your own abilities. It may be that you are not yourself luminous, but you are a conductor of light. Some people without possessing genius have a remarkable power of stimulating it. I confess, my dear fellow, that I am very much in your debt.”

He had never said as much before, and I must admit that his words gave me keen pleasure, for I had often been piqued by his indifference to my admiration and to the attempts which I had made to give publicity to his methods. I was proud, too, to think that I had so far mastered his system as to apply it in a way which earned his approval. He now took the stick from my hands and examined it for a few minutes with his naked eyes. Then with an expression of interest he laid down his cigarette, and carrying the cane to the window, he looked over it again with a convex lens.

“Interesting, though elementary,” said he as he returned to his favourite corner of the settee. “There are certainly one or two indications upon the stick. It gives us the basis for several deductions.”

“Has anything escaped me?” I asked with some self-importance. “I trust that there is nothing of consequence which I have overlooked?”

In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes says some people, though not geniuses, have a rare power to bring out the best thinking in others. Good company sharpens good work.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes builds his picture of a stranger one step at a time from a dated stick and a checkable career, asking at each step what the evidence actually allows.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, the old family manuscript argues that no burden is so heavy it cannot be lifted by repentance. The wise response to the past is to learn from it, not to be ruled by fear of it.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, the captive woman escapes by her own nerve, climbing down the ivy in her fear. Courage often shows up when someone has no good options left.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, the old manuscript notes that what is clearly known carries less terror than what is only hinted at and guessed. Plain facts calm people more than rumour does.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, the one detail nobody can explain is the change in the footprints past the gate. The loose end that refuses to fit is often where the truth is hiding.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Dr. Mortimer shows how a family legend, taken too much to heart, can wear a sensible man down until fear shapes every choice he makes.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes works out how long a man stood still from a small, checkable fact: ash dropped twice from a cigar. Small details often carry more weight than the big picture.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes says he works only within this world, yet insists a footprint is material evidence that must be explained. Stay with what can be examined, and do not wave away the awkward fact.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes points out that the world is full of obvious things that nobody ever actually notices. Attention, not cleverness, is most of the detective's craft.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes insists that every ordinary explanation must be exhausted before anyone reaches for the strange one. Ask first whether a crime happened at all, and then how it was done.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes tests a theory by asking whether the behaviour makes sense: a frightened man should run toward help, not away from it. A story is coherent only when every fact fits.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes shows that expertise is seeing differences others walk past. Each specialist finds the obvious in their own field, and that trained eye is what makes the work look like magic.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes says the detective balances probabilities and picks the most likely explanation, using the imagination but always starting from some material fact.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Sir Henry answers a warning by asking whether the danger comes from a legend or from people, then refuses to be scared off his own home. Courage that asks the right question is worth having.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes owns his own mistake plainly, going through what he should have done instead. Naming where you went wrong, without excuses, is how you get better at the work.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes can switch his mind off a hard problem for hours at a time and lose himself in art. Stepping fully away from a puzzle is often how the next clear idea arrives.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes says a hard case is worked by following several threads, knowing some will waste time, until one leads to the truth. Keep following the threads.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes cannot go himself, so he recommends the friend he trusts most to stand beside a person in danger. Knowing your own limits and asking a steady friend to help is good judgement.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Conan Doyle has Holmes refuse to treat a run of strange, apparently pointless incidents as separate accidents. He sets himself to find the scheme that joins them.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes admits plainly that he is uneasy about sending Watson into danger, and says he wants his friend back safe. Honest worry is a kind of care.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes refuses to drop suspects early. Clearing the innocent is an injustice, and dismissing the guilty loses the chance to prove it, so he keeps everyone on the list until the evidence settles it.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson reads Sir Henry's eagerness and bearing and judges him a companion who would bravely share a risk. Character often shows in how a person meets the world.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Sir Henry will not drop a household that has served his family for generations just because he is new. He values long loyalties and does not treat people as disposable.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson notices that a small detail, the butler's denial against the woman's red eyes, does not add up. He follows the mismatch instead of brushing it aside.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson tests each explanation against the whole pattern. A simple motive does not fit a web of schemes, and he admits he has not reached a conclusion yet.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson sets aside his wish to stay by his friend's side, because the papers there are not his to help with. He takes up the task that was actually asked of him.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson presses Miss Stapleton to be frank with him. When everything feels full of shadows, he says, a plain answer is worth more than a polite one, and he asks for it directly.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson reads Stapleton by his looks and by how he keeps his sister and the baronet apart, not by his polite words. Actions and small signs often tell more than speeches.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Frankland fights lawsuits for the pleasure of fighting and takes either side. Watson shows how a quarrel pursued for its own sake can cost a man his whole fortune.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson keeps what he saw apart from what he suspects. He reports the watching figure at the window and admits he cannot yet say what it means.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson admits his first suspicion was a love affair and reports it anyway, though it proved unfounded. He would rather show his reasoning and be wrong than keep it to himself.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson's conscience will not let him excuse himself after letting Sir Henry go out alone. He sets off at once to put it right rather than make his failing sound reasonable.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Doyle shows that a plain, honest admission can disarm anger. Watson owns up to following Sir Henry, and the baronet's temper gives way to a rueful laugh.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Stapleton's apology shows how fear of losing someone can drive a person to say things they later see as selfish, especially when that person has been their only company.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Doyle shows two men keeping a long, dull watch through two nights. Patient interest, the kind a hunter brings to a trap, is what keeps them going when nothing happens.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Mrs Barrymore explains how her family indulged her brother until he thought the world existed for his pleasure. Indulgence, she shows, can grow into a harm nobody meant to cause.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Sir Henry admits his fright and asks Watson for the plain truth. Naming a fear openly, Doyle suggests, makes it easier to face.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson insists on common sense, yet admits that facts he cannot explain still deserve a fair hearing. He weighs both explanations honestly instead of picking the one he prefers.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Sir Henry separates a truth given freely from one forced out of someone. Doyle shows that the way a confession comes out can change how it is judged.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson pities the escaped convict out on the cold moor. He concedes that whatever the man's crimes, he has suffered something to atone for them.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Mortimer judges a woman's ruin fairly, saying the fault may not have been entirely on one side. Several neighbours then quietly helped her earn an honest living.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson reasons that a formal visit would yield less, so he goes alone and keeps things low-key. Doyle shows that the way you approach people shapes what they will tell you.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson tests a witness's story against what could practically have happened. He reasons that a secret trip that hard to hide was unlikely, so part of her account is probably true.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Frankland wins every quarrel with his neighbours and still calls it a red-letter day. Doyle shows how a passion for being in the right can crowd out any real peace.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson finds that a meek admission he has spoken without all the facts pleases the old man and loosens his tongue. Doyle shows that humility can open a door that argument keeps shut.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson swears that he will not miss his chance through lack of energy or perseverance. Doyle makes the point that a lead is worth little unless someone is willing to follow it through.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes keeps Watson in the dark because a friend who knows too much can give the game away without meaning to. Doyle shows that discretion can be a kind of care.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes traces Stapleton through a true story he once told about his own past. Doyle shows how a person often gives themselves away through an honest remark made without thinking.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes takes his share of the blame for a client's death instead of hiding behind the facts. Owning a failure plainly is part of being trustworthy.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes will not act on suspicion alone. He insists the case must be proven, because one false move can let a clever villain escape.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes keeps pressing on the detail that does not fit, such as how the convict knew the hound was after him. Anomalies are worth chasing.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Barrymore's wife grieves for a man the world sees only as violent, because to her he is still the boy she once knew. The people closest to us often see more than strangers do.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes says his eyes are trained to look past a face's trimmings, such as dress and disguise. Looking past surface details is a basic skill of good judgement.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes lays out his evidence openly and invites the witness to check him for error. A fair test of a case is whether the other person can find a mistake in it.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes keeps his plans to himself until the moment they unfold, and his helpers find this trying. Caution can be sound, but a leader who hides the plan from the team wears them out.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Watson's terror breaks once he sees the monster can be wounded. Fear often shrinks when you find out what the thing is actually made of.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Mrs Stapleton, long controlled and abused, chooses to tell the investigators the truth and help them. Breaking free of a manipulative person can take real courage.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes sees that Stapleton could hide his hound but could not hush its voice. Every scheme has a weak point that gives it away, and finding that point is often how a case is solved.
In The Hound of the Baskervilles, Holmes keeps an indexed list of his cases because new work crowds out memory. Written records are how a busy mind holds on to what it has learned.
